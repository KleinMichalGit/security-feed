South African clothing retailer Foschini Group investigating cyberattack on British fashion brands

news The Record · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

The South African retail giant The Foschini Group (TFG) disclosed a cyberattack on Tuesday that disrupted IT systems at three of its British fashion brands, leaving some services unavailable.

The incident, discovered Sunday, affected Phase Eight, Hobbs and Whistles, which operate under the group's UK division, TFG London. The company said its stores remained open, but several IT systems were still offline as the specialists worked to determine the extent of the breach.

TFG has not identified the attackers or disclosed how they gained access to its systems. It also has not established whether any personal or sensitive information was compromised.

In a statement to investors published through the Johannesburg Stock Exchange's news service, the company said it had reported the incident to relevant UK authorities and was working with cybersecurity specialists to investigate.

The company's two other British brands, Inside Story and White Stuff, were not affected. Its operations in South Africa and Australia also remained unaffected because they use separate IT systems.

In an email to customers cited by local media, TFG warned that orders placed around the time of the incident could be delayed and advised shoppers against submitting duplicate orders.

The company said payments were processed by an external provider and that it had no reason to believe payment information was at risk. It also said customers did not need to change their account passwords at this stage.

TFG said it would notify affected customers or organizations if its investigation found that sensitive information had been compromised.

Headquartered in Cape Town, TFG is one of South Africa's largest retail groups, with nearly 5,000 stores across 18 countries and a portfolio of roughly 40 brands spanning fashion, beauty, jewelry and homeware.

The company’s online sales were also affected earlier this year by a cyber incident involving “a key online concession partner,” TFG disclosed in its June financial results.

Its London division reported a more than 65% drop in earnings, with the partner's cyber incident cited among the contributing factors. There is no indication that the earlier disruption is connected to the latest attack.

The incident comes amid continued cyber threats to major fashion and retail brands.

Earlier this month, British online fashion retailer ASOS said attackers had compromised an employee account by impersonating a trusted contact, allowing them to send an unauthorized push notification to customers.

In August, American apparel maker Levi Strauss & Co. and Dutch luxury department store chain De Bijenkorf also reported cybersecurity incidents.

Last year, major retailers including Marks & Spencer, Harrods, Louis Vuitton, Mango, and the Co-op Group disclosed cyberattacks or data breaches.

'123456' password linked to Denmark data breach affecting 8.8M people

news The Register · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

A person claiming responsibility for the breach of Denmark's Central Register of Persons (CPR) says they gained access through an IT company's account protected by the password "123456." The person told Danish news outlet Politiken they had no intention of selling or leaking the data. The newspaper reported that at least three company accounts, including an administrator account, used the same password. Denmark's CPR administration confirmed a breach affecting the information belonging to 8.8 million people on October 5, adding that a Danish company's legal access to the CPR system was abused. It did not name Funen-based Pays ApS as the company in question, but managing director Sophie Laursen confirmed on October 9 that it's access had been exploited. Pays' website has been taken offline, and the business has not responded to The Register's request for more information about the password claims. Pays had authorized access to the CPR system to provide services such as validating business customers' records. The Danish government says information relating to 8.8 million people was accessed. The register contains approximately 11 million records, including those of deceased people and people who have moved abroad. The government did not respond to questions about the alleged password failings. According to documents obtained by broadcaster TV 2, the attacker gained access to the CPR system on September 10 and ran the 14 million automated searches between then and September 20. The intrusion was detected on October 2, 12 days after the last reported searches. Mikkel Leihardt, director of the government's higher education and science ministry, told reporters at a press conference that the attack was discovered after a department staffer picked up an invoice indicating unusually high activity from Pays. XWorm RAT Evolves Into Surveillance Platform

news SecurityWeek · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

The XWorm RAT developers have expanded the malware’s capabilities with fresh plugins that turn it into a surveillance platform, cybersecurity firm Netskope reports.

A sophisticated, multi-functional Windows RAT first observed in 2022, XWorm provides attackers with full control over the infected systems, offering keylogging, screenshot capture, webcam access, and payload execution capabilities.

The malware’s developers have been constantly updating its codebase and adding new functionality, and the threat has remained highly active, with Netskope observing over 1,200 incidents across 100 organizations during the third quarter of the year.

Alongside new variants, the developers have also increased the number of plugins XWorm supports, from 35 in July 2025 to 46 in October 2026.

Some of these have been clearly designed for surveillance: enabling location tracking, live audio streaming, webcam capture, and interactive control over authenticated browser sessions.

“The Maps plugin provides victims’ GPS location without a user prompt. The Microphone plugin streams live audio from the victim’s device. HBrowser hijacks authenticated browser sessions without touching credentials, bypassing MFA entirely. Webcam capture is also available on demand,” Netskope says.

According to the cybersecurity company, the activity surrounding XWorm has increased since a cracked version of its builder was released publicly in June 2025, and the source code for RAT’s 6.4 version was leaked in August 2025.

With the addition of the HBrowser plugin, the RAT moves beyond harvesting credentials and user data from browsers: it provides the operator with a real-time view of the browser and can relay attacker inputs to the browser.

Operator’s interactions are encoded as command-and-control (C&C) commands, transmitted over the standard XWorm AES-encrypted communication channel, and executed on the victim’s system.

“The victim machine receives them and executes the corresponding input on the active browser window. The operator receives a continuous stream of screenshots. From the operator’s perspective, it is a full remote browser session. Every authenticated tab is accessible, bypassing the need for logging in and dealing with MFA,” the company explains.

The Maps and Microphone plugins allow operators to monitor the victim’s location and to capture audio without privilege escalation and without a user warning.

“XWorm continues to evolve and has now added surveillance capabilities. Since its builder and source code were leaked, we expect continued upgrades and use of this toolkit,” Netskope notes.

Attackers Heat French Supermarket Fridges Up to 50°C by Hijacking Danfoss Controllers

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

Attackers took remote control of fridges and freezers at hundreds of French supermarkets on the night of October 11 and warmed them, forcing stores to throw food away, several retail chains said.

The attackers reached them through the controller that runs all of a store's fridges and freezers, a device made by Danish company Danfoss that maintenance firms can manage remotely. Les Mousquetaires, which owns the Intermarché chain, said its fridges and freezers were put into defrost and heated to as much as 50°C.

CERT-FR, the French government's computer emergency response team, said in an alert published in French on October 12 that it is aware of numerous compromises affecting two Danfoss controller series, the AK-SM and the AK-SC. It told operators to disconnect the controllers or filter access to them.

CERT-FR is part of ANSSI, France's national cybersecurity agency. ANSSI's head, Vincent Strubel, called it urgent to take the two series off the internet or filter access very tightly, AFP reported.

Neither French authorities nor Danfoss has so far said who carried out the attack or how the attackers got into the controllers. Danfoss said most of the units hit were older models that it no longer updates.

Which Chains Were Hit

The chains gave their own figures to AFP, Reuters and broadcaster RTL. Each counts the stores the attack reached.

Retailer Stores Reported Hit Source
Intermarché and Netto (Les Mousquetaires) At least 300 across both brands, per AFP. About 150 stores, per Reuters Company statements, October 12
Coopérative U 80 of 2,000 Chief executive Dominique Schelcher, on RTL, October 13
Carrefour 80 supermarkets Company statements to AFP and Reuters, October 12
Monoprix About 30 Company statement to AFP, October 12
E.Leclerc Several stores Company statement to AFP, October 12

A store on that list did not always lose its stock. Schelcher told RTL that the cold chain held in most of the 80 Coopérative U stores hit, and that only a minority could not react fast enough and lost goods, according to AFP. He praised the quick reaction of store staff and refrigeration contractors.

Intermarché, by contrast, said its affected stores had to throw away all the products concerned.

Thierry Cotillard, who heads Les Mousquetaires, told broadcaster BFMTV he expected losses of tens of millions of euros across France, according to Reuters.

In its statements on October 12, Danfoss gave no figure for how many systems were hit. It told Reuters it was "aware of incidents in France affecting a limited number of refrigeration management systems."

Which Controllers, and Whether They Can Be Patched

Danfoss told AFP that most of the units hit were older products that no longer get software or security updates, and it named two: the AK-SM 800 and the AK-SC 355.

Danfoss declared the AK-SM 800 end-of-life in May 2021 and phased out the AK-SC 355 in 2016. In an October 2025 newsletter, it said it would end all services for such older products and urged customers to plan replacements.

Some units of the current model, the AK-SM 800A, were also hit. Those were running outdated firmware, according to Danfoss's statement to trade publication Cooling Post, which did not name the versions.

Controller Status, According to Danfoss Advice From CERT-FR and Danfoss
AK-SM 800 End of life since May 2021. No software or security updates Disconnect it or filter access. Danfoss urges replacement
AK-SC 355 Phased out in 2016. No software or security updates Disconnect it or filter access. Danfoss urges replacement
AK-SM 800A Current model. Package 5.3.x, dated October 2026, was the latest listed on October 13 Disconnect it or filter access. Danfoss advises keeping firmware up to date

As of October 13, Danfoss's security advisories page listed no advisory about the attack or about either older model.

What Operators Should Do

CERT-FR's first instruction covers every AK-SM and AK-SC controller: disconnect it or filter access to it. Exposing a management interface to the internet goes against good practice, the alert says.

Where remote administration cannot be avoided, limiting access to trusted IP addresses is a first, temporary step. Beyond that, the alert advises following CERT-FR's hardening recommendations, which were written for the energy and water sector.

Where a controller may have been compromised, the alert lists six steps, in this order:

  1. Limit network access to the controllers to service providers only.
  2. Do a factory reset.
  3. Apply all available security fixes.
  4. Change the device's secrets, such as passwords.
  5. Check the existing accounts.
  6. Reconfigure the device completely.

Danfoss no longer issues fixes for the AK-SM 800 and the AK-SC 355, so the third step offers their owners no new fix.

For the AK-SM 800A, the latest software package Danfoss listed on October 13 was 5.3.x. A system on version 3.x should move to 4.x first, and a controller cannot be downgraded once it runs 5.3.x, according to the package's readme.

Starting with version 4.0, a setting called Strict Session Control is enabled by default and makes HTTPS mandatory. Danfoss's pages do not say whether the 5.3.x package was released in response to the attack.

Danfoss told Cooling Post it was working with customers on "firmware updates where available, stronger network protections and removing direct internet exposure where applicable."

Its general security guidance, published in French, also tells operators to keep controllers unreachable from the internet, change default passwords when a system is installed, and require remote users to log in through a single managed entry point.

CERT-FR had published no indicators of compromise as of October 13, so operators have no official signs to check for.

Coopérative U's stores do not buy directly from Danfoss. They are customers of refrigeration contractors that supply them with the Danfoss system, Schelcher said.

How the Attackers Got In Is Not Established

As of October 13, CERT-FR had named no vulnerability, no firmware version, and no way in. Its alert warns against exposing management interfaces to the internet without saying that was the route.

A person close to French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu told AFP that ANSSI had identified a vulnerability in software supplied by a Danish company and had contacted the vendor. No CVE was named, and the statement does not say the vulnerability was used in the attack.

E.Leclerc told AFP that a cyberattack had affected one of its refrigeration equipment suppliers, with effects on cold equipment in several of its stores. The supplier was not named.

Danfoss issued fixes for three flaws in the newer AK-SM 800A in 2025. Neither French authorities nor Danfoss has linked them to this attack.

CVE-2025-41450 is an authentication bypass, a way past the login, in versions before 4.2. CVE-2025-41451 is a command injection flaw that could lead to remote code execution, and CVE-2025-41452 could allow a denial-of-service attack. Both need a login and were fixed in version 4.3.1.

Researchers at Claroty described the three flaws in August, said they could be combined into attack chains, and reported finding thousands of AK-SM 800A management interfaces reachable from the internet. Their tests of the flaws were laboratory work on the newer model. The report describes no attacks and does not mention the two older models.

Danfoss's October 2025 newsletter records earlier attacks on the AK-SM 800: 16 Russian supermarkets using the model were targeted in 2022, and 40 Israeli stores were hacked the same year. All 40 lacked proper firewall or VPN protection, and the newsletter names no vulnerability in either case.

The Inquiry and Reports From Other Countries

The Paris prosecutor's office has opened an inquiry on its own initiative, it told AFP. Its cybercrime section leads the inquiry, which is assigned to the anti-cybercrime office known as OFAC.

The inquiry is to count the victims and establish the link between the attack and the damage they reported, which includes lost food and equipment that began to catch fire.

ANSSI is in contact with cybersecurity agencies in other European countries because those countries may be affected, the person close to the Prime Minister said.

Félix Aimé, a senior threat intelligence analyst at Sekoia, told Reuters that Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany and Belgium had also been affected. Reuters gave no details on those cases.

Two Critical MarkLogic Flaws Could Give Delegated Managers Persistent Admin Control

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

Two of 31 newly disclosed flaws in Progress Software's MarkLogic Server database could let an account given only some management privileges take administrator control of the server, the company says.

Progress published all 31 on October 12 and scored five of them critical. Fixes have been available since September 10, in MarkLogic 11.3.7 and MarkLogic 12.1.0.

Affected releases, and the release that fixes each:

  • MarkLogic 11.x before 11.3.7: upgrade to 11.3.7
  • MarkLogic 12.x before 12.1.0: upgrade to 12.1.0

MarkLogic 11.3 is the long-term support (LTS) line. The 12 line, which Progress calls an innovation release, has no patched 12.0 release. Its fix is 12.1.0, which also adds new features.

Installing the fixes for the 10 MarkLogic flaws Progress disclosed in August is not enough. Those fixes arrived in 11.3.6 and 12.0.3, and both releases are affected by the new flaws.

Progress's CVE records and its upgrade notes for the two releases do not say whether any of the 31 flaws has been used in attacks. None was in the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog when The Hacker News checked on October 13.

The same records and notes list no workaround for customers who cannot upgrade yet.

What the Critical Flaws Allow

Progress scored three of the five critical flaws 9.9 out of 10 on the CVSS scale and the other two 9.6. For each one, the attacker must already have a privilege or a way to run code on the server, according to the company's CVE records.

Two of them involve forests. In MarkLogic, a forest is a collection of documents that is attached to a database.

CVE Score What the Attacker Needs What It Could Allow
CVE-2026-85947 9.9 Forest-management privileges Create a persistent administrator account, potentially giving complete administrative control of the server
CVE-2026-85948 9.9 Manage-user-level privileges Gain persistent administrative control, through an XQuery injection flaw in a deprecated Management API path
CVE-2026-85955 9.9 Query-execution access and the xdmp-eval-in privilege Run server-side code with elevated permissions
CVE-2026-85956 9.6 Delegated database-administrator privileges Back up or restore forests outside its own database, allowing access to or changes to what Progress calls "security-critical data"
CVE-2026-9191 9.6 The ability to run code through certain REST and XDBC evaluation paths Perform unauthorized actions or compromise an authenticated session

The fix for CVE-2026-85947 shows which accounts are at issue. A "forest-scoped administrative grant" is no longer enough on its own to attach a forest to a database of the account's choosing, the upgrade notes say. They also tell customers to review delegated forest and database privilege assignments before upgrading, and to audit the roles that hold xdmp-eval-in.

Outside researchers reported four of the five through Bugcrowd. A researcher using the handle rexnets is credited with CVE-2026-85947, CVE-2026-85948, and CVE-2026-85956, and another using the handle WaltWhitehat is credited with CVE-2026-9191.

Flaws That Depend on Configuration

Progress scored 24 of the remaining flaws high and two medium. The highest, CVE-2026-85954, at 8.6, could allow an attacker to gain more privileges than granted by passing values to certain XQuery functions.

Several of the others apply only where a named feature or platform is in use:

  • SAML: In deployments that use SAML authentication, an unauthenticated remote attacker could make the server accept an assertion it should reject (CVE-2026-85962). On 11.x, the SAML parser could also process external DTDs or entities (CVE-2026-85957).
  • OAuth: An attacker could use a token that the server should not accept (CVE-2026-85958). The fix adds issuer and audience checks for Ping Identity and other OAuth configurations.
  • Password-based Kerberos: In certain configurations, an attacker positioned on the network could impersonate the Kerberos authentication service (CVE-2026-85972).
  • Cloud hosting: An authenticated attacker could access internal cloud services and obtain cloud credentials on Azure (CVE-2026-85951) and on Linux-based cloud deployments (CVE-2026-85952).
  • Diagnostic logs: Under certain configurations, the server could write security-sensitive information to diagnostic logs (CVE-2026-85949 and, on Azure, CVE-2026-85961). AWS secret keys and session tokens were written in plaintext when the affected IAM trace path was enabled, the upgrade notes say.
  • Amazon S3: Connections to S3 could use cleartext by default (CVE-2026-85975).
  • Query Console: A remote attacker who gets a logged-in Query Console user to open a malicious page could run server-side code with that user's permissions (CVE-2026-85960). An authenticated Query Console user could read arbitrary server files (CVE-2026-85964).
  • Legacy components: OpsDirector, a deprecated feature, could let an unauthenticated remote attacker make requests to internal services or forge trusted requests (CVE-2026-85963). Legacy InfoStudio modules could give access to server files (CVE-2026-85953).

The rest include three memory-safety flaws, a cross-site scripting flaw, HTTP response splitting in Query Console, inconsistent TLS hostname checks on some outbound connections, and cleartext cluster-management traffic on 12.x.

What the Upgrade Changes

Progress tells customers to read its upgrade notes, the known incompatibilities for 11.3.7 and those for 12.1.0, before upgrading. The 11.3.7 notes list behavior changes tied to 22 of the flaws, and the 12.1.0 notes to 21.

Changes that can break existing code, tools, or automation include:

  • mlcp: Older versions of MarkLogic Content Pump (mlcp) fail against the new releases with an XDMP-ISOLATED error. Progress recommends mlcp 11.3.7 or 12.1.0. Older versions can run with -restrict_hosts set to true, which can reduce performance.
  • Removed features: The /manage/v2/packages API, InfoStudio, and OpsDirector are removed.
  • New defaults: XML external entity processing is off by default, S3 connections default to HTTPS, and new and rotated ErrorLog.txt and AuditLog.txt files are created with mode 0640 instead of 0666.
  • Privileges: xdmp:eval-in() now needs both the xdmp-eval-in and xdmp-eval privileges. Reading Authorization headers needs a new privilege that no built-in role holds. Forest attachment, backup and restore are checked against every forest and database they touch.
  • Stored secrets: FlexRep configuration responses no longer return stored passwords, client private keys or passphrases.
  • Platform: MarkLogic 11.3.7 does not support Amazon Linux 2.
  • Name conflicts: The upgrade can fail if the cluster already has a role, user or privilege with one of five names used by a new ticket system. The names are in the upgrade notes.
  • Changes with no CVE attached: Both releases limit XML entity expansion and drop weak TLS ciphers from the defaults. On 12.1.0, Optic fragment queries return only document fragments by default.

On the fix for CVE-2026-85954, the notes warn: "As a result, you may no longer have privileges that you previously had access to, because that access was incorrect."

Some problems remain after the upgrade. Existing ErrorLog.txt and AuditLog.txt files keep their old permissions until they are rotated or recreated. The Kerberos fix needs a keytab, and without one, the server logs a warning and skips the new check.

Another flaw, CVE-2026-85959, meant successful logins were not always recorded in the audit log. Progress says that could limit a customer's ability to detect and investigate unauthorized access or credential misuse.

The CVE records and upgrade notes do not say whether upgrading removes an administrator account created through CVE-2026-85947 before the upgrade.

Second Batch Since August

The 31 flaws are the second batch Progress has disclosed for MarkLogic in 68 days. On August 5, it published 10, seven of them scored critical. One, CVE-2026-9192, was scored 9.8 and allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to bypass password verification on the ODBC App Server, according to its CVE record.

Both times the fixes shipped first. The August flaws were fixed in releases dated July 1 and July 6, 35 and 30 days before their CVE records appeared. This time the gap was 32 days.

A search by The Hacker News of the U.S. National Vulnerability Database (NVD) on October 13 returned 49 CVE records that mention MarkLogic, 41 of them from these two batches. The other eight, from 2017 and 2018, are for Antenna House DMC HTMLFilter, a third-party component used by MarkLogic 8.0-6. Earlier MarkLogic releases also carried security fixes: the release notes for 11.3.1, 11.3.2 and 11.3.4 each mention "patches for security vulnerabilities" and name no CVE in the server's own code.

VMware is pushing so many patches, it thinks users need new tools to manage the flood

news The Register · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

VMware has discovered so many flaws in its products using AI that it felt the need to create a new product to help users manage them. Prashanth Shenoy, vice president of product marketing in the VMware Cloud Foundation (VCF) division of Broadcom, told The Register that VMware recently moved to deliver monthly software updates, in part to address bugs found by AI. He admitted, however, that users struggled to keep up with the pace of change before the move to monthly releases because it takes time to test the impact of patches – even though VCF allows non-disruptive live patching. Shenoy said customers therefore need "room to breathe." Enter a new tool called HyperArmor, which does three things. One is "virtual patching" – blocking attempts to exploit a vulnerability without changing the vulnerable software itself. VMware already offers virtual patching in its vDefend network security product and explains how it works by referring to CVE-2024-5806, the bug in file transfer tool MOVEIt that made waves in 2024. VMware says vDefend can detect patterns associated with attempts to exploit the authentication bypass flaw and block the traffic. HyperArmor will do similar things to block malicious traffic heading toward VCF implementations. Another job HyperArmor takes on is rejecting any actions that alter known good states in VCF. If a human approves a change, the tool will update policies to allow the altered state. The third job is multi-factor authentication (MFA). The Register put it to Shenoy that MFA is now table stakes for security. He agreed but explained that VMware designed HyperArmor to protect against attackers using stolen privileged credentials to access VMware's ESX hypervisor. To (hopefully) stop attackers using those creds to take over a host, HyperArmor requires users to input SSH credentials and a secondary time-based one-time password sent to a user with a verified identity. HyperArmor is a discrete product that VMware will sell as an add-on to VCF. That means VMware thinks its customers are going to be OK paying extra for MFA. While VMware will sell HyperArmor, it didn't create the product. Broadcom's security businesses Symantec and Carbon Black cooked it up. Broadcom's CA business (formerly Computer Associates) has done similar things. At VMware Explore on August 31, Broadcom launched AgentMinder, which aims to stop AI running inside VCF going rogue. AgentMinder came from CA but was launched as a tool for VCF (and third-party Kubernetes stacks). At VMware Explore, The Register met Ram Velaga, president of Broadcom's Infrastructure Software Group, and asked him if the group has a single strategy, or if its divisions each do their own thing. Velaga said Broadcom uses a combination of both approaches. "There's a level of focus with individual teams that you don't have if you have one very large central team," he said. "You get the speed at which you have to innovate with individual teams." Broadcom's Infrastructure Software Group therefore operates with at least six teams, and when one of those teams wants to add functions it will look for existing assets developed by another internal team rather than trying to write its own code. For broad products like VCF, the work of several Broadcom teams could therefore be relevant. Prominent Ransomware Negotiator Faces Extortion Charges

news Dark Reading · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

US authorities last week charged a ransomware negotiator over alleged extortion activity — the latest example of cybersecurity professionals being implicated in the very activity they're supposed to defend against.

Edward Dubrovsky, 54, was charged for conspiracy to threaten to impair the confidentiality of information with the intent to extort money, under 18 U.S.C. § 1030(a)(7)(B), a federal provision of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA).

While the exact context of the charge remains unclear, as the main complaint is still sealed, what is particularly notable about this case is Dubrovsky's prominence within the security community as both an executive and an established ransomware negotiator. Prior to these charges, he regularly shared his expertise with various publications, including Dark Reading. He also recently self-published a strategic guidebook discussing best practices for responding to cyber-extortion attempts.

Based in Canada, Dubrovsky is associated with a number of cybersecurity firms. He previously was an executive at Cypfer, an incident response firm, from 2022 until the end of 2025, and is a member of the advisory board at FileFlex, a zero-trust product vendor. He also recently attended a cybersecurity conference in Philadelphia with a firm he said he currently works with, cybersecurity advisory firm CyberSteward.

Dark Reading reached out to Cypfer, CyberSteward, and FileFlex parent company Qnext for comment, but none responded by press time.

Possible ShinyHunters Connection

None of the publicly available documents involving Dubrovsky tie his arrest to a specific incident. However, FBI Director Kash Patel said in a post to X Friday that the bureau "arrested another suspected co-conspirator of the ShinyHunters group — the group believed to be responsible for the recent FBIjobs.gov incident, which occurred on a platform managed by a third-party vendor."

While Patel did not identify the suspect, several factors suggest Dubrovsky may be the individual referenced in the post. A Philadelphia judge sent Dubrovsky's case to Texas, where a source told KrebsOnSecurity recent ShinyHunters investigations are centralized. Furthermore, The New York Times on Friday reported that the FBI arrested a Canadian man in Pennsylvania suspected of working with ShinyHunters.

ShinyHunters is a loose collective of threat actors known for data extortion attacks. The group previously was behind a number of highly visible threat campaigns involving compromised Salesforce instances and attacks on higher education institutions.

But perhaps most notably, ShinyHunters last month hacked a third-party jobs site used by the FBI. The group further claimed they stole data belonging to nearly every FBI employee and job applicant. In response, the FBI has aggressively pursued ShinyHunters, making a number of arrests in the process.

Other Legal Issues in the Ransomware Negotiation Industry

Dubrovsky is not the first ransomware negotiator charged in connection with his work. Last week, Zohar Pinhasi was arraigned by the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of New York on wire fraud charges for allegedly defrauding clients of his ransomware remediation company, MonsterCloud. Pinhasi was accused of promising clients that he could decrypt ransomware without paying criminals, only to then pay criminals himself and charge the end customer a substantial markup.

What to do when a data breach notice doesn't say much

Data breach notifications are telling victims less than ever. What the company leaves out can still shape what you do next.

12 Oct 2026  •  , 7 min. read

Publicly reported data breaches hit an all-time high in the US last year, with over 3,300 incidents recorded and nearly 280 million victim notices mailed out. But are all of these worth the paper they’re written on? Increasingly, they lack crucial details.

As the volume of data breach incidents increases, there’s a growing chance that you’ll be caught up in one. According to the non-profit Identity Theft Resource Center (ITRC), over 471 million victim notices went out in the first half of 2026 alone. Yet just a quarter (24%) contained details about the attack vector. That’s the lowest ever rate recorded by the ITRC.

This is bad news for consumers, corporate victims and policymakers. A lack of detail prevents you from understanding your true risk exposure or taking meaningful action to limit the fallout and possibly prevent it from happening again. ITRC’s own data shows that three-quarters of victims want a plain-language list of exactly what data was compromised.

Why are organizations cagey about releasing details?

There are many reasons why a breached entity might be reluctant, or unable, to share the full details of a breach incident. Crisis comms teams may want to get some information out to customers once news of an incident surfaces, to preserve reputation and prevent harmful speculation from mounting. But they may not yet know the full picture.

Information such as exactly what was compromised and how may take weeks or even months to establish, especially if it involves large data volumes. And if there’s a chance that broader disclosure could help other threat actors (by revealing the attack path and defensive weaknesses) or the adversary itself (if it is still inside the network) then it may be withheld.

There are also more financially driven motives for limiting disclosure. Excessive info-sharing might impact the share price, reputation, and/or competitive advantage of an organization.

The truth is that disclosures to regulators will usually feature far more detail. The victims, meanwhile, are often left grasping at straws.

How do you interpret a breach notice?

The language used in a breach notification can be confusing. If it says your data was “accessed” it doesn’t necessarily mean it was actually stolen (i.e., “exfiltrated”). And just because a breached company claims they have seen “no evidence of misuse” there’s absolutely no guarantee that this is true. What they see and what’s actually happening on the cybercrime underground can be two very different things.

Similarly, if a company says you “may have been affected” it doesn’t mean your data was definitely in the trove that was compromised – although it’s always best to err on the side of caution.

As mentioned, breach notices may be cagey about exactly how the hackers got in and stole the data (the attack vector). But for victims, this is one of the less important pieces of information. What you really need to know is:

  • Whether your data was ‘only’ accessed or actually taken (although it’s best to assume the worst until you hear different)
  • What data was taken, as this will determine your next steps (e.g., whether you need to reset passwords, call your bank, cancel cards etc)
  • When the incident happened and how long it lasted, as this can help you to check against suspicious activity on affected accounts
  • What the company itself is telling you to do in terms of specific next steps

Is the notification even legitimate?

It’s also important to check if the notice is genuine. Scammers have grown wise to the fact that breach notifications are an increasingly common occurrence. And something that recipients are likely to take notice of and act on.

For that reason, you should never click through on any unsolicited breach notification email or message. Sometimes the stated incident might never have happened. On other occasions the original breach might be real but the notification message is a scam.

In any case, your first port of call should be to check with the breached organization. Do this not by replying to the message or using contact details listed there, but verifying them elsewhere.

What should happen next?

Once you’ve confirmed the notification is legitimate, ask the organization involved if it can clarify any more details. Then consider the following steps, which are dependent on the type of data that may have been compromised:

  • For personal details like email and home address, name and phone number, be on the lookout for follow-on phishing messages and calls. Never click on links in these messages or hand out information without verifying their authenticity separately
  • For health info or customer account details, watch out for more targeted phishing attempts as per the above
  • For usernames and passwords, change the credentials immediately for this account and anywhere else it was used. Switch on two-factor authentication (2FA) for an extra layer of security
  • For bank or card details, contact your bank or card provider and tell them what happened. Ask for a replacement card and monitor your accounts for suspicious activity
  • For government ID (e.g., passports, driver’s license) follow the advice of the issuer. A replacement may be needed
  • For financial and identity info, monitor your accounts, contact the relevant financial institution(s), change your logins (if relevant), and consider credit monitoring and a credit freeze

Is a credit freeze or credit monitoring the best option?

Many breached organizations offer free credit monitoring for a year following an incident. Some also offer a credit freeze. But which is better? It’s important to understand the difference.

  • Credit monitoring services detect when something changes on your credit file, potentially indicating a fraudster has your details and is trying to get a new line of credit out in your name.
  • Credit freeze blocks most access to your credit report, so scammers can’t easily open a new account using your details.

So a credit freeze is more useful, but if either is offered, take advantage of them. The two work best together: place a freeze and accept any free monitoring you’re offered. Just be aware that credit monitoring is only an alert service. It won’t actually prevent follow-on fraud.

As more of us expand the number of organizations we do business with, and as hackers get better at compromising those same companies, the odds of receiving a breach notification will continue to shorten. But it doesn’t have to be the end of the world. Stay alert and stay informed to minimize the risk of follow-on fraud.

Frequently asked questions (FAQs)

How do I know if a breach notification is genuine or a scam?

Never click through on any unsolicited breach notification email or message. Check first with the breached organization, and not by replying to the message or using contact details listed there, but verifying independently.

Why have I received a breach notification from a company I’ve never heard of?

It may be a supplier you’ve never dealt with directly, such as a payroll provider or data processor used by a company you do business with. It may also be a scam, or you may simply not have done business with them for a long time and it could be a historic breach. Either way, read the information carefully and contact the company directly for more information if it looks like sensitive financial or personal information was exposed.

What should I do if my password was exposed in a breach?

Change the credentials immediately for this account and anywhere else it was used. Switch on two-factor authentication (2FA) for an extra layer of security.

What should I do if my financial details and identity information were exposed in a breach?

This is more serious. Monitor any relevant accounts for suspicious activity and contact the relevant financial institution(s). Change your logins (if relevant) and consider credit monitoring and a credit freeze.

Should I use credit monitoring after a breach?

Credit monitoring services detect when something changes on your credit file, potentially indicating a fraudster has your details and is trying to get a new line of credit out in your name. It won’t actually prevent follow-on fraud, but it can be a useful alerting mechanism. If offered for free, accept it. A credit freeze is worth adding: it’s free in the US and blocks most access to your credit report.

CloudSyncD: macOS malware analysis

CloudSyncD first stage: Password prompt

The dropped file is known as the CloudSyncD family, based on the application it masquerades as cloudsync. The file is a Mach-O universal binary that contains multiple architecture slices bundled together (ARM64, x86_64), allowing for maximum compatibility across victims. This first stage primarily loads the embedded payload.

CloudSyncD incorporates string obfuscation through an in-house library, based on its symbols (cloudsync_impl::sobf, class template CTEnc<N>). Strings are encrypted at compile-time and decrypted at runtime, either through CTEnc<N>::str() or a standalone helper (sobf::reveal) that implements the same algorithm. Each string’s ciphertext is decrypted byte by byte with a custom XOR-based cipher using the following formula:

plain[i] = enc[i] ^ K[i & 0x1F] ^ K[seed & 0x1F] ^ ((17*i - 125*seed) & 0xFF)

Here, K is a fixed 32-byte table embedded in the binary and shared by every string, and seed is a per-string key byte, either stored with the ciphertext (CTEnc<N>) or passed as an argument (sobf::reveal).

Final stage: Information stealer

The DonutLoader-shaped shellcode deploys a native information stealer. The executable adds a protection layer that rearranges its main code region into 16-byte blocks, while a small restoration stub remains intact at the entry point: 

How the block-shuffle protection restores itself

In our analyzed sample, the protected region begins at the start of the .text section at 0x140001000 and extends to 0x140224940, which is the start of the recovery stub and is the last function in the .text section. This covers about 140,180 blocks. The blocks don’t necessarily align with instructions or function boundaries, therefore producing misleading disassembly that hinders static analysis.

The entry/recovery stub reconstructs the code through the following sequence:

1. Locate the protected region:

A call $+5/pop sequence obtains the stub's current address. The code then calculates the protected region's location (0x140001000) relative to that address.

2. Derive the permutation seed:

The stub computes a 64-bit FNV-1a hash over its first 15 bytes and bytes 23–244 (end of the stub). The eight bytes at offsets 15–22 are excluded because they contain an encoded seed. XORing the encoded seed with the calculated hash produces the linear congruential generator's initial state to be used in step 3:

Encoded seed: 0x3BB3350249EF1EC2 Stub hash: 0x98B6991CE2443C4F Recovered LCG seed: 0xA305AC1EABAB228D

This ties correct restoration to the stub's exact bytes: modifying a hashed instruction changes the recovered seed. This also means that setting a software breakpoint anywhere in the stub results in an incorrect restoration at runtime.

3. Advance to the end of the generator sequence:

The stub initializes a deterministic 64-bit number generator with the recovered seed. Each update multiplies the current state by A (0xF7A874C6B9DE43D5), adds C (0xE0ED02C1BBE35BB7), and retains only the lowest 64 bits. It performs 140,179 updates to reach the state needed to begin reversing the block-shuffle sequence, since the restoration loop must traverse the generator's states backward:

state = seed Repeat block_count − 1 times: state = (A × state + C) mod 2^64

4. Restore the original block order:  

Starting with block 1, the stub takes the generator state's upper 31 bits and reduces that value to an index between zero and the current block number. It then exchanges the two 16-byte blocks.

After each iteration, the generator moves backward by subtracting the original increment and multiplying by the inverse constant, A_inverse (0xF40FBF0EDD42DD7D), again retaining only the lowest 64 bits. The stub continues through the remaining blocks, reconstructing the original code order. Iterations that select the current block leave its contents unchanged but still update the generator:

for i = 1 through block_count − 1: j = (state >> 33) mod (i + 1) if i != j: swap block[i] and block[j] state = ((state − C) × A_inverse) mod 2^64

How Chrome App-Bound Encryption is handled

The malware includes collectors for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Yandex. The Chrome branch implements a multistage process to recover protected browser keys and read profile databases. It also extracts saved passwords, session cookies, browsing history, download records, and profile metadata.

Before it can reach the key material, the collector performs four privilege-related operations:

  • Enumerate browser data directories under C:\Users.

  • Enable SeDebugPrivilege, SeBackupPrivilege, and SeRestorePrivilege. These requests activate privileges already present in the process token and don’t independently grant administrative access.

  • Duplicate a SYSTEM-context impersonation token from lsass.exe or winlogon.exe.

  • Search running processes for a token belonging to the browser profile’s owner, using token information and SID-to-account lookup to identify a matching user.
