Torvalds: AI is fine for competent devs, but spare us the 'drive-by' patches

news The Register · 2026-10-23 · ↗ original

Linus Torvalds is embracing AI to write Linux kernel code, and some people are having fits. He has a message for them. So do I. Roy Schestowitz, editor of Tux Machines, summed up his reaction to the news as "Linus Torvalds Turns to Slop." In the podcast Pivot to AI, podcaster David Gerard agreed: "Linux kernel goes full AI slop code." Meanwhile, in the Tom's Hardware comments section, commenter "LordVile" wrote: "This is a publicity stunt from his AI overlords [The Linux Foundation] who pay his wage." They followed this by saying this is the "same reason he's flipped-flopped on AI." I've known Linus for a few decades, and I don't take his use of AI as evidence that he's suddenly abandoned his standards. And the very idea that he'd do anything he didn't want to do with Linux on behalf of the Linux Foundation, or anyone else, is ludicrous. I get it, though. There are reasons to be suspicious of AI. I've written about many of them. For instance, AI model collapse, which happens when AI engines learn from other AI models and their ties to the truth fray. It's a very real problem. Not, mind you, that LLMs find the grist for their answer mills from very reliable sources in the first place. Even when AI gets good data, as it does in programming, its owners pay little to no attention to the copyrights and licenses protecting the open source code it picked up its coding tricks from. As for paying for the information it runs on, please! The very idea that Big AI must pay writers, artists, you name it, for using their work is anathema to them. All that said, when it comes to programming, the ship has sailed. AI is becoming part of the programming toolkit, whether its critics like it or not. Why? Because while you still can't vibe code your way into delivering production-quality software, when someone who knows what they're doing, like Torvalds, uses AI, it can be very useful. Anyone who says otherwise is just making the same mistake the Luddites did. Who now, some of you may ask? They were textile workers who attacked machinery in the early 19th century in protest against changes threatening their wages and livelihoods. You may have noticed that while people still weave cloth by hand, industrial production is mechanized. The same is happening now with AI and programming. I think we're about halfway through the process, and by 2030 or so, people programming by "hand" will be about as common as weavers doing it by hand are today. In short, get over it. I don't love it, either. I write for a living, and my business is being crunched too. At least in programming, quality work still counts, no matter how it's done. With writing, though, true AI slop is increasingly the rule, not the exception. Back to Linux: at the Linux Kernel Maintainers Summit in Prague the other week, Sasha Levin, a Linux kernel developer, announced that the maintainers had decided to "deprecate the Assisted-by Tag." That means you no longer need to say if you used AI in your code submissions. That doesn't mean you can turn in slop, though. That code won't pass muster. As Torvalds told me in a note the other day: "I think the whole 'don't use AI for commit messages' kind of argument … Maybe it's well-intentioned, but I think it's entirely misguided. I really think we want competent people to use AI as a tool." Competent is the key word there. Torvalds also wrote: "The 'clueless people using AI' problem is different. I do think we want to discourage that. Not discourage the people who use AI and truly want to learn and truly want to become useful members of the kernel community – but discourage the drive-by patches, and discourage people who just want to feel like they sent a patch and never really wanted to become anything more. We all start clueless, but some people spend the time and effort to become something more." Exactly so. As Torvalds said at Open Source Summit Europe: "I think [vibe-coding is] a wonderful way to find joy in programming. It makes you feel like you're doing something relevant. It allows you, as a new programmer, to do things that you would otherwise have a really hard time with." He added: "I love the concept of AI as a gateway drug" to programming. Besides, AI has already proven very useful for improving Linux code. As long-term Linux kernel maintainer Greg Kroah-Hartman told me back in March: "Months ago, we were getting what we called 'AI slop,' AI-generated security reports that were obviously wrong or low quality." But then, "something happened a month ago, and the world switched. Now we have real reports." It's not just Linux, he continued. "All open source projects have real reports that are made with AI, but they're good, and they're real." True, the maintainers are getting buried in good bug reports now, but as Kroah-Hartman and discussed in Prague, AI bug finders might eventually exhaust the readily discoverable problems, easing the pressure on maintainers. Another AI win is that you can use AI to reverse-engineer Windows drivers for Linux more easily. Christian F.K. Schaller, Red Hat's director of software engineering, created hw-harness while working on a Linux driver for a slide scanner. This open source project provides "tooling for working out how a piece of hardware behaves so that you can write a Linux driver for it." By automating some seriously slow, tedious ways of figuring out what a binary-only driver was doing with the hardware, Schaller has made building Linux drivers from Windows drivers much easier. I know, because I did this myself back in the mid-2000s, and those manual methods are still what people use today. This could go a long way toward fixing one of those lingering annoyances of the Linux desktop: poor or entirely missing hardware drivers. In the meantime, Torvalds told me: "Well, in all fairness, we do clearly have an 'AI problem.' Or rather, we have two independent AI problems: we have a large codebase that AI finds real bugs in, and that adds a lot of work on already fairly overworked maintainers. And then we have people who use AI to generate patches without actually understanding the code in question." He continued: "But I'd argue that these aren't problems with AI itself; they are just problems that are exposed by AI. They are very much both very real problems, and I understand that people can really come to dislike – and even hate – AI due to them, but I do think the underlying issue isn't AI itself." It's how people misuse AI. In the right hands, as the top Linux programmers are showing us, AI can be useful. ®

Researchers Demonstrate Node2Shell RCE Exploit on Node.js Build Using Crafted Brotli Data

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-23 · ↗ original

Swati KhandelwalOct 23, 2026Vulnerability / Web Security

Malicious Brotli data can make an application on an official build of Node.js run attacker-supplied code, security researchers at Imperva have demonstrated.

Their "Node2Shell" proof of concept ran code on the official Node.js 24.22.0 image for Linux arm64, with no debugger or custom build, by feeding crafted data to the bundled Brotli decoder (CVE-2026-111497). Node.js disclosed the flaw and released fixes on October 21, and applications that decode untrusted Brotli data should update.

Imperva's test did not succeed on every attempt, and code execution took several tries in its Linux arm64 setup. The result covers that one build and application setup, with other architectures and the 22.x and 26.x lines not tested. On the fixed release, 24.22.1, the same data was rejected, and no code ran.

The demonstration goes beyond the first public proof of concept, which achieved code execution only on a deliberately weakened test build. On a standard build, that proof of concept's author reported a crash and a limited read of process memory, not code execution, and said a working attack on a normal build was not achieved.

No exploitation of the flaw in the wild has been reported as of October 22.

Which Applications Are Affected

The flaw is in the Brotli decoder that Node.js bundles, so it affects applications on the 22.x, 24.x, and 26.x lines, not only the one build Imperva tested. A service reaches that decoder from either side of an HTTP exchange: when it reads a Brotli-compressed response through Node.js's built-in fetch(), or when middleware decodes a Brotli-compressed request body.

Express 5's body parsers, such as express.json(), express.urlencoded(), express.text() and express.raw(), now decode Brotli request bodies when applied to a route, alongside gzip and deflate. A plain Node.js HTTP server does not decode a Brotli request body just by receiving one, so the decoding needs that middleware or an explicit call.

Imperva reproduced code execution through both the request-body and response paths, testing Express 5.0.0 to 5.3.0 and body-parser 2.0.0 to 2.3.0 at pinned versions, and did not test every release in between.

How the Bug Works

Imperva traced the cause to how the decoder handles an impossible point in a Brotli stream: the stream announces the block types it will use as decoding runs, and the test input reached a block change that the stream's own structure ruled out. The vulnerable decoder read that as a request for more input and kept going in an invalid state.

In a separate test with a memory sanitizer, continuing from that state wrote past an intended buffer, the out-of-bounds write behind the flaw.

Node.js fixed the flaw by importing an upstream Brotli patch that makes the decoder stop with a format error at that point instead of continuing.

What to Do

The fix is to move to a patched release. The update should reach container images and other artifacts that bundle a Node binary, which a package inventory can miss. End-of-life lines 20.x and earlier do not receive the fix and should be moved to a supported line.

The advisory lists no workaround inside Node.js, so an application that cannot update at once has no fallback in the runtime. Imperva suggested limiting untrusted Brotli traffic on affected paths where that is practical, and said only the update removes the flaw. The company also added rules to its own web application firewall and tested them against the attacks it reproduced.

Vercel, which runs Node.js for its customers, said it added rules on October 21 that block the attack in inbound requests. The rules do not cover Brotli responses an application fetches from other servers, and do not replace updating.

Medical laboratory operator Quest Diagnostics said it learned of a cyber breach of one of its systems this week, likely compromising some records for patients.

In a Thursday regulatory filing, Quest Diagnostics reported “unauthorized access” to its Quanum Lab Services Manager, a platform where certain clients can place test orders and receive or look up their results. The company said it discovered the hack on Wednesday and notified federal law enforcement.

How many patients and which particular records have been affected is still unknown. Quest Diagnostics said it believes “certain patient records” were accessed in the breach, but that the full scope remains under investigation.

Toronto crime tips org freezes operations after details of data breach come to light

news The Record · 2026-10-22 · ↗ original

A Toronto crime prevention program that long promised witnesses a safe space to anonymously report tips froze its operations late Wednesday night, hours after a Canadian news outlet reported that more than 1.5 million tips from Crime Stoppers chapters across the country were exposed in a data breach.

Crime Stoppers urges people not to provide their names when submitting information, but reporters at Canada’s CBC broadcaster were able to reach more than a dozen tipsters by reviewing the hacked data made available to them in March by Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets). 

About half of the 1.5 million leaked tips CBC studied — some dating back 25 years — contained personal information, the outlet reported.

While Canadian Crime Stoppers Association and individual chapters, including Toronto’s, had confirmed the breach in the days after the hack, they offered no detail about what was exposed, blaming the lack of information on vendor Navigate360 and its subsidiary, P3 Global Intel.

But late Wednesday night the Toronto chapter broke ranks with the national association, revealing a timeline showing what it knew and when along with an announcement that it will pause operations.

“Since 1984, people in Toronto have come to Toronto Crime Stoppers because we promised they could stay anonymous,” Sean Sportun, chair of Toronto Crime Stoppers, said in a statement. “This week’s reporting shows that for some of them, that promise was broken. We are deeply sorry.”

The Toronto organization said it has directed Navigate360 to immediately stop taking tips and no longer process any new information. It also has told Canada’s National Anonymous Call Centre to stop receiving and processing tips meant for Toronto Crime Stoppers, according to the statement.

“Until we have a safer way to protect you, please do not send us tips by phone, online, or through the app,” the statement said.

The organization is looking for a way to take tips on a truly anonymous basis, it said.

“For more than 40 years, people in Toronto have relied on Crime Stoppers to share what they know without giving their name: a witness afraid of retaliation, a neighbour who can’t be seen talking to police, someone reporting a person close to them,” the statement said. 

“Their courage has helped solve crimes, prevent tragedies, and save lives. Pausing our program means that option isn’t available now, and we did not make this decision lightly. We are pausing because those people deserve a system we can fully stand behind.”

Canadian Crime Stoppers Association and Navigate360 did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Navigate360 told Toronto Crime Stoppers that the stolen data contained 176,859 Toronto tips, which were reported between January 2022 and November 2025, the Toronto chapter’s statement said. The vendor also told Toronto Crime Stoppers that it had found 14,973 names in those tips, but could not say if the names belonged to tipsters, suspects or both.

Toronto Crime Stoppers has made “numerous requests” through their lawyers for the text of the stolen tips, but Navigate360 has not responded, the statement said. 

“Since March, Navigate360 has told us repeatedly that P3 is secure,” the statement said. “In April, it told us Toronto’s data had not been taken. When it told us in June that Toronto’s data was affected, it didn’t say what was exposed.”

A hacktivist collective known as Internet Yiff Machine took responsibility for the March breach, saying its goal was to prove that Navigate360 and P3 Global Intel lied about their capabilities.

Splunk Enterprise Patroni REST API Missing Authentication Vulnerability

CVE-2026-76268 is a critical missing authentication vulnerability in Splunk Enterprise. An unauthenticated attacker with network access to the Patroni REST API on a search head cluster member could execute attacker-controlled operating-system commands. Splunk rates the vulnerability 9.8 (Critical) under CVSS v3.1. Its advisory does not report active exploitation. Splunk Vulnerability Disclosure A public repository contains code presented as a proof of concept for this CVE. GitHub

Technical Details

The vulnerability exists because the Patroni REST API does not require authentication for critical configuration operations. An attacker who can reach the affected interface on a search head cluster member could abuse those operations to execute operating-system commands.

  • Weakness: CWE-306, Missing Authentication for Critical Function.
  • Attack vector: Network access to the affected Patroni REST API.
  • Authentication: No credentials or existing privileges required.
  • User interaction: None required.
  • Impact: Operating-system command execution, with potentially high impact to confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
  • CVSS v3.1: CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H

Internet exposure is not required. An attacker with internal network access to the affected interface could also exploit the vulnerability.

The Current Landscape of OAuth Attacks

threat-intel Unit 42 · 2026-10-22 · ↗ original

As enterprises continue to roll out multi-factor authentication (MFA), attackers have adapted. Rather than only stealing credentials, attackers have shifted to stealing Open Authorization (OAuth) tokens, the keys that govern what applications can do on your behalf. Adversaries are already accelerating these attacks by using dynamic evasion code, phishing-as-a-service (PhaaS) templates and LLM-generated brand impersonations.

Because OAuth attacks occur at the authorization layer after user authentication, stolen access and refresh tokens bypass MFA, survive password resets and grant durable access.

While OAuth misuse occurs at a lower volume than traditional phishing attacks, these attacks may carry severe consequences such as:

  • Account takeover
  • Persistent access to sensitive data that survives password resets and MFA re-enrollment
  • Lateral movement across every service connected to the compromised identity
  • Data exfiltration without triggering credential-based alerts
  • A foothold in enterprise environments for broader supply chain or insider attacks

Defending against these threats can be accomplished using several strategies:

  • Disabling end-user app consent for vague or impersonating app names
  • Enforcing admin-only approval workflows
  • Monitoring authorization code clipboard events
  • Continuously auditing registered redirect_uri values

To understand why OAuth has become such a prime target for modern adversaries, it helps to examine the authentication challenges the protocol was originally designed to solve. Looking back at how applications handled identity before OAuth reveals how the threat landscape has evolved from simple password theft to complex token hijacking.

The Past: Phishing for Passwords

Websites and applications often tie together functionality from other sites. For example, some popular ecommerce and social media platforms allow users to log in through their email accounts.

In 2006, before OAuth, combining functionality between applications involved sharing one application’s credentials, which are supposed to be secret, with another. This type of sharing is a significant security issue because it increases the consequences of a successful credential phishing attack.

In credential phishing, attackers trick users into sharing their login credentials, allowing attackers to gain full access to their accounts. This could be access to an email account, business system, social media account or bank account. The attacker gains access to all of the information in that account and the authority to make changes, including altering the password and locking out the user.

During the pre-OAuth era, applications or sites sharing credentials meant that attackers could pivot to different platforms or services through compromising a single account.

To address these issues, the OAuth working group released the first draft of OAuth Core 1.0 in 2007. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) released Request for Comments (RFC) 6749 for OAuth 2.0 in 2012 and is working on OAuth 2.1 as of 2026.

The Present: Why OAuth Became the New Attack Surface

The OAuth Framework

OAuth allows users to grant third-party applications passwordless authorization to their data on another service. Figure 1 displays a sample sign-in screen presenting OAuth authentication options with three different Identity Providers (IdPs): Google, Apple and Microsoft.

In a typical OAuth flow:

  1. The user authenticates with the identity provider
  2. The user approves the permissions (i.e., scopes) requested by the third-party application
  3. The identity provider issues an access token to the third-party application
  4. The third-party application then uses the access token to retrieve user data within the authorized scopes

This token is temporary, revocable and has scoped permissions. This gives the third-party application access to permitted resources on the user’s behalf.

OAuth Becomes the New Attack Surface

Standard phishing attacks are designed to steal login credentials like user account names and passwords. OAuth-based phishing bypasses login credential authentication entirely.

Victims authenticate through legitimate IdPs and complete MFA. They are then redirected with tokens that can grant attackers persistent, durable access.

Attackers don’t need to defeat MFA directly, because they are able to bypass it by acquiring the authorization tokens after a victim authenticates. This presents a weak spot between authentication and authorization where OAuth-based phishing can happen. If someone is the victim of a phishing campaign, their stolen tokens may give unintended permissions to an attacker’s app.

A large-scale analysis of OAuth websites found that 18.53% of these sites request permissions beyond the minimum required (typically just a user's profile and email). While not directly malicious, this statistic illustrates the likelihood of privacy and privilege leaks when excessive permissions are granted.

The Core Vulnerability: Tokens Outlive Passwords

One of the primary advantages of OAuth is that tokens, unlike passwords, are designed to be short-lived and easily revoked. However, in practice, OAuth tokens:

  • Are frequently configured to be long-lived
  • Survive a password reset since the token is not a password
  • Bypass MFA since the victim already completed MFA during the flow
  • Provide persistent access (until explicitly revoked) since refresh tokens silently mint new access tokens for days or months after the initial compromise

This persistence directly amplifies the following authorization vulnerabilities:

  • Cross-User OAuth Session Fixation (COSF)
  • Cross-Tenant Client ID Confusion
  • Cross-Connector OAuth Account Takeover

COSF allows an attacker to use a long-lived token as a permanent backdoor. This type of vulnerability arises when an OAuth session is used to complete an authentication flow without verifying its strict binding to the initiating user’s identity.

  1. The attacker initiates an OAuth process (e.g., “Connect your Slack”) inside a target app
  2. Before completing the OAuth process, the attacker freezes the session and sends the victim a phishing link
  3. The victim logs into their real Slack account, but through the attacker's fixated session
  4. The resulting access token lands in the attacker's account

The next two vulnerabilities rely on OAuth-as-a-service (OaaS). OaaS lets organizations outsource token management to a centralized cloud provider where the provider creates a single token vault and manages tokens for hundreds of different apps (tenants). These are commonly used by organizations with many applications. OaaS architecture is inherently susceptible to Cross-Tenant Client ID Confusion and Cross-Connector OAuth Account Takeover.

Cross-Tenant Client ID Confusion

In Cross-Tenant Client ID Confusion, a malicious app (Tenant A) swaps its app identifier (Client ID) to impersonate a trusted app (Tenant B) sharing the same vault. The vault trusts both tenants, processes the request under the trusted app's context and accidentally exposes access to the malicious tenant.

Cross-Connector OAuth Account Takeover

In Cross-Connector OAuth Account Takeover, a malicious tenant manipulates how the vault maps OAuth sessions to connectors. A victim's authorization flow, meant for a different app entirely, gets rerouted into the attacker's control. As a result, attackers take over the full account without stealing credentials

Both attacks get easier the longer a token stays valid. A short-lived token limits the damage window.

Existing Mitigations

OAuth security is decentralized and dictated by the IdP. While major IdPs have robust defenses against flow misuse, many smaller platforms offer weak protections.

The root challenge is the lack of a global registry for Client IDs. Without a universal ground truth of identity, attackers can easily impersonate legitimate app names on lax platforms. To counter this, leading IdPs enforce strict guardrails. Google, for example, mitigates misuse through:

  • Domain ownership verification for redirect URIs
  • Unverified app warnings to deter users from unauthorized integrations
  • Strict security audits for apps requesting sensitive scopes

Because you cannot rely on every IdP to protect your data equally, a third-party consent security policy is essential. A centralized, automated policy enables organizations to standardize their defenses by:

  • Enforcing least-privilege
  • Blocking unverified apps
  • Revoking risky grants uniformly, regardless of the individual provider's security gaps

OAuth-Based Phishing Campaigns

Currently, OAuth-based phishing campaigns fall into three categories, each defined by what the attacker is trying to achieve:

  • Account hijacking: The attacker obtains a durable token granting them the same access as the victim (full identity assumption)
  • Permission hijacking: The victim is tricked into consenting to a malicious app, giving the attacker scoped access to specific resources
  • Malicious redirect: The attacker uses the OAuth flow's redirect mechanism to deliver malware or phishing, exploiting the trust users place in flows that originate from real identity provider domains

Account Hijacking: Stealing the Identity Itself

Account hijacking is the most complete form of identity compromise. The attacker ends up with an OAuth access or refresh token that authenticates them as the victim on every downstream service that trusts that identity provider. Attackers misuse two distinct OAuth flows to achieve account hijacking: the device authorization flow and the authorization code flow.

Device Authorization (DeviceAuth) Misuse

How the Legitimate Flow Works

The device authorization grant was designed for devices that cannot open a browser such as smart TVs, gaming consoles and command-line tools. The device displays a short code and a URL (like microsoft[.]com/devicelogin). The user opens that URL on a separate device, enters the code, signs in and the original device receives an access token.

How Attackers Exploit It

Instead of a legitimate device requesting the code, the attacker's server requests it. The attacker then shows that code to the victim and instructs them to paste it into, for example, Microsoft's real device login page. When the victim signs in and submits the code, they are unknowingly authenticating the attacker's session.

Authorization Code Theft via Benign Apps

How the Legitimate Flow Works

In the standard authorization code flow, a user authenticates with an identity provider, which issues a short-lived authorization code and redirects the browser to a registered redirect_uri. The application then exchanges that code for an access token in a server-to-server call the user never sees.

How Attackers Exploit It

Attackers use a legitimate application's redirect_uri to expose the authorization code in the browser, then use social engineering to extract it. No malicious app registration is needed; the attack rides on apps that already pass every security check.

Permission Hijacking: Tricking Users into Granting Access

Permission hijacking is more of a surgical attack than a full account takeover. Rather than stealing a code or device token, the attacker's goal is to have the victim click Allow on an OAuth consent screen that grants an attacker-registered application persistent, scoped access to specific resources. The victim authenticates legitimately and completes MFA. They simply consent to the wrong application.

The consent screen is the attack surface. Victims reach it through two attack strategies: vague app names or brand impersonation.

Vague App Names

In this strategy, the attacker registers an application with an innocuous-sounding name (e.g., Privacy Policy Extension or Security App) that provides no meaningful signal about its intent. It then requests permissions far beyond what the name implies.

Brand Impersonation

In this strategy, the attacker registers an application that displays as a trusted brand while the underlying client_id belongs to an attacker-controlled application. The consent screen shows the name provided at registration rather than any verified identity. As such, the victim sees a familiar brand name from a legitimate identity provider, with no visible signal that the application is fraudulent.

Malicious Redirect: Weaponizing the OAuth Flow for Delivery

In malicious redirect attacks, the attacker's goal is not account access or a scoped token. Instead, they weaponize the OAuth authorization flow itself as a delivery mechanism, exploiting the trust users place in pages and redirects that originate from real identity provider domains.

Attackers have used different tactics to weaponize the OAuth flow, such as:

  • Impersonating trusted services
  • Triggering OAuth errors
  • Leveraging less safe IdPs

Impersonating Trusted Services

Attackers register OAuth applications that impersonate trusted services and configure attacker-owned domains as the redirect_uri. When the victim completes the OAuth flow on the real identity provider, the browser is redirected to the attacker's domain as part of the normal protocol. Because the user just interacted with a legitimate identity provider and expects to land somewhere, the redirect is implicitly trusted.

Triggering OAuth Errors

Documented by Microsoft in March 2026, a more covert campaign exploits how identity providers handle errors to redirect users to attacker-controlled malicious landing pages. Attackers crafted authorization URLs designed to deliberately trigger an authentication error. For example, they combined prompt=none with parameters that required user interaction, creating an irresolvable conflict.

When the identity provider cannot complete the authorization, it does not show an error page. Instead, it appends error parameters to the redirect_uri and sends the browser there automatically. An example of a redirect URL format after triggering an error is as follows:

<attacker-domain>/security/?state=<encoded-victim-email>&error=interaction_required&error_subtype=access_denied

Since the crafted link points to a trusted OAuth provider, the user is more likely to click it and be exposed to the malicious content with just that one click.

Leveraging Less Safe IdPs

Attackers weaponize lax validation on less secure IdPs to execute redirection attacks. For example:

  • If an IdP fails to validate the presence of a client_id, attackers can weaponize the authorization server as a generic open redirector without even creating a malicious OAuth app
  • If an IdP has a permissive redirect_uri validation, attackers may leverage path traversal or URL parameters to redirect to their own malicious domains

The Escalation: Shift From Basic to Sophisticated Methods

Credential phishing generally relies on mass-delivered, low-fidelity landing pages designed to capture plaintext passwords. Today, attackers have evolved from only harvesting static secrets to hijacking active, authenticated sessions. By positioning themselves inside legitimate OAuth protocols, campaigns can bypass perimeter controls, turning the trust mechanisms of single sign-on (SSO) and federation into vectors for compromise.

From Static Pages to Dynamic Evasion

Security architectures have in the past heavily relied on static analysis and domain reputation to block malicious links. In response, threat groups shifted from static HTML templates to dynamic, highly evasive client-side execution environments.

Key characteristics of this shift include:

  • Runtime decryption: As observed in recent campaigns, threat actors deliver payloads as obfuscated blobs (using encryption algorithms such as AES-GCM) via edge infrastructure like Cloudflare Workers. The payloads assemble the interface only after execution in the victim's browser. Figure 2 below shows a code snippet from an example of a phishing page.
  • Active environment fingerprinting: Phishing pages now query the browser environment, implementing heuristics to detect developer tools, automated sandbox configurations or headless security crawlers.
  • Defensive evasion loops: When the payload detects a monitoring tool or security analyst is detected, the page shifts its behavior, invoking endless debugger loops or silently routing traffic to legitimate domains to preserve the longevity of the infrastructure. Figure 3 shows an example of code used for this type of monitoring.

The PhaaS Economy

The effectiveness of these campaigns is amplified by the commercialization of specialized threat kits within the cybercriminal underground. We observed that the attackers use the same template to generate OAuth phishing pages impersonating different brands. We show examples of different brand impersonations in Figure 4.

This PhaaS ecosystem has two key characteristics:

  • Modular templates: PhaaS platforms now offer plug-and-play OAuth components. A single underlying framework can be dynamically re-skinned with different corporate assets. We have observed attackers impersonating multiple popular document sharing platforms as part of campaigns.
  • Shared infrastructure: Multiple seemingly distinct campaigns share identical tokens. We have observed identical data used for anti-bot tokens (example in Figure 5) and Cloudflare Real User Monitoring (RUM) tokens. This indicates that specialized developers are renting highly capable infrastructure to a broader array of less technical affiliates.

LLMs as an Attack Accelerator

Generative AI has lowered the barrier of entry for executing high-fidelity, targeted identity operations. We have observed signs of websites generated with the help of LLMs, such as the example below in Figure 6. The code in these phishing pages suggests that attackers are using LLMs to impersonate legitimate pages and rapidly deploy attacks.

The operational advantages provided by LLMs include:

  • High-fidelity brand impersonation: Attackers leverage LLMs to overcome typical signs of impersonation, such as poor syntax, unnatural phrasing or layout inconsistencies. Generative models enable adversaries to rapidly produce context-specific localization, creating more convincing brand imitations that can mislead users.
  • Rapid deployment cycles: Rather than relying solely on static templates or manual development, threat actors can leverage LLMs to generate functional landing page code tailored to specific target organizations. This allows attackers to build targeted, localized campaigns and test and deploy attacks at a velocity that reactive defense layers struggle to match.

Expanding OAuth Ecosystem

OAuth adoption continues to grow beyond identity providers into infrastructure platforms, and every new platform that joins the ecosystem adds a new consent surface for attackers to exploit. A notable recent example is Cloudflare's June 2026 launch of self-managed OAuth clients, which now allows third-party developers to build applications that act on behalf of users to access their Cloudflare accounts.

Cloudflare accounts control critical infrastructure such as DNS, CDN routing and edge compute. This means a token granted through a malicious OAuth app carries a far greater impact radius than one granting access to email or files. An attacker who tricks a developer into consenting could gain the ability to deploy malicious pages, redirect legitimate traffic or build phishing infrastructure on domains that most enterprise security tools inherently trust.

How to Defend Against OAuth Attacks

Standard perimeter and client-side security controls fail against OAuth phishing attacks, particularly for the following reasons:

  • Access tokens survive a password reset
  • The attack bypasses MFA because the victim has already completed MFA during the legitimate flow
  • Conditional Access and IP firewalls fail because OAuth grants access to any token presenter, regardless of origin

Unlike a typical login where a server might establish a persistent, multi-factor session bound to a specific user's browser, the server validates the token's cryptographic signature. However, the session lacks an inherent link to the user's initial authorization context. Additionally, because flows like device authorization intentionally decouple the initiating and authenticating devices, enforcing strict IP or device consistency checks would break the protocol's design.

Effective defenses must focus on account hijacking, permission hijacking and malicious redirects.

Defenses for Account Hijacking

Attackers rely on manipulating victims into pasting sensitive codes into legitimate portals such as device login pages or messaging applications. Security tools can monitor for clipboard copy and paste events along with the source and target of these events. For example, if code is copied from an untrusted source, the tool can flag this anomaly and alert the user before token generation occurs.

Defenses for Permission Hijacking

Organizations can combat consent phishing through strict name-checking and mandatory user consent rerouting. Security posture management tools must enforce policies that automatically block consent requests from apps with vague or impersonating names. Ultimately, enterprises should disable end-user consent for third-party integrations entirely, routing all requests through an admin consent workflow where security teams can review and prune excessive scopes before authorization.

Defenses for Malicious Redirects

To prevent attackers from weaponizing OAuth callback URLs to deliver malware or steal credentials, organizations must mandate redirect destination audits. This requires a continuous, automated inventory of all registered applications to ensure every configured redirect_uri points to verified domains. Any app attempting to register an external, recently created or unmanaged domain as a callback target must be automatically quarantined to neutralize redirect exploitation.

User awareness is the final line of defense against social engineering. Employees must treat OAuth consent screens with the same scrutiny as password fields. They should not grant permissions to apps with vague names or excessive scopes and avoid copy-paste authorization or device codes into unverified chat applications.

Conclusion

OAuth-based phishing represents a fundamental shift in our threat landscape. Attackers no longer need to steal credentials when they can harvest tokens that bypass MFA entirely and persist through password resets.

Defending against this threat requires visibility where the attack happens:

  • In the browser
  • At the moment of authorization
  • Before a token is ever issued

Organizations that wait for credential-based alerts or rely solely on post-authentication controls will find themselves unable to see these attacks. In our next blog, we’ll discuss how OAuth attacks are expected to evolve, especially with AI agents.

Overview of the intrusion

A squirrel in the world tree

The word Yggdrasil originates in Norse mythology, where it refers to a sacred tree connecting the various realms of the cosmos. Within that tree lives a squirrel named Ratatoskr that carries messages between its top and its roots. Given the backdoor’s role as a messenger scurrying through the Yggdrasil network, we decided to name the backdoor MythicRatatoskr.

More technically, Yggdrasil is a decentralized mesh network that connects devices directly to each other without a central hub, building an encrypted IPv6 overlay on top of the internet so that participating nodes can exchange end-to-end-protected traffic. Legitimate users adopt such meshes for resilient connectivity, research, and hobby networking. Because no single server runs the mesh, the network is robust and hard to disrupt – properties that also make it attractive to adversaries. Importantly, Yggdrasil is not designed to provide anonymity.

Network protocol

To communicate with the C&C server, MythicRatatoskr relies on the Yggdrasil network – a decentralized mesh network in an alpha state that implements an experimental routing scheme. Each node in the network generates its own Ed25519 key pair, which allows protected end-to-end IPv6 communication between the peers. The IPv6 address is derived from the public key in the 200::/7 subnet range, which was deprecated by IETF in 2004. Anonymity is not among the goals of the project. We explore this topic more in the Network infrastructure section.

The yggdrasil-go repository already provides the necessary types and methods to join the Yggdrasil network. There’s also another project, called yggstack, that enables the Yggdrasil network to communicate via a network stack in the user space. This has the benefit of not needing to create a tunnel interface, which might be an issue if the user does not have the required privileges to create one, or if such an interface is not supported on the system. The MythicRatatoskr code is based on yggstack, making the malware portable.

There’s a publicly available network that one can join by adding at least one public peer into the Yggdrasil configuration. MythicRatatoskr specifically contains an embedded base64-encoded blob that contains a list of public peers in the URI format. Most peers on the list are still operational. MythicRatatoskr randomly picks three peers from the hardcoded list, which is the recommended number, and connects to them in order to join this public Yggdrasil network.

Once MythicRatatoskr is part of the Yggdrasil network, it communicates with its C&C server, which is also part of the network, via HTTPS on port 443.

Communication uses JSON-encoded HTTP POST requests containing a uuid (explained in Table 4, in the MythicRatatoskr main configuration section), a message type, and optional data. Four message types exist:

  1. checkin – register the backdoor and send victim metadata such as username, hostname, and local IPs; the server responds with a fresh uuid and psk),
  2. tasking – poll for new commands,
  3. result – return command output, base64-encoded when present,
  4. and getfile – retrieve file content for the upload command.

Although the C&C server is hosted in the Yggdrasil network – which may prevent network security tools from detecting outbound connections to it – blocking the public peer addresses is a viable mitigation.

Configuration

We distinguish between two types of configuration:

  1. a backdoor-specific configuration used by the malware itself; and
  2. an Yggdrasil configuration used to join the mesh network.
MythicRatatoskr main configuration

The MythicRatatoskr configuration is stored in a file at a hardcoded path. The path can differ in each sample; we identified the following paths:

  • C:\Windows\Temp\.updatecache (in krbtman.exe, Act64.exe, ie.exe, ieupdate.exe, and windowsdefendersvc.exe)
  • C:\Windows\system32\.updatecache (in updater.exe, and windowsdefendersvc.exe)
  • %LocalAppData%/Temp/.crond (only in OneDriveLiveUpdater.exe)

The %LocalAppData%/Temp/.crond path is notable, since such a filename (.crond) along with forward slashes is expected for Unix-like systems. This indicates that a Linux variant (or macOS, FreeBSD, etc., though less likely) of MythicRatatoskr may also exist. Moreover, OneDriveLiveUpdater.exe contains an unused configuration path /tmp/.test_config; the filename suggests that this configuration path may have been used during testing and accidentally left in a Windows deployment.

All of the configuration filenames start with a dot. On Unix-like systems, programs used for listing files, such as ls, typically exclude these entries by default, effectively hiding them. This is not the case on Windows, where these files are not hidden. This further suggests the existence of a Linux variant.

Yggdrasil configuration

Initially, when MythicRatatoskr starts, it generates a new Yggdrasil configuration, including a new Ed25519 private key. Because the Yggdrasil IPv6 address is derived from the public key, the victim machine’s Yggdrasil IPv6 address changes each time the malware runs.

MythicRatatoskr then uses empty NodeInfo data and sets the NodeInfoPrivacy flag. As a result, other nodes in the Yggdrasil network won’t be able to get more information about the node, such as the Yggdrasil version or the operating system on which the node is running.

The backdoor uses a publicly available Yggdrasil network to reach the C&C server. To join the network, it needs to connect to at least one of the publicly available peers. All MythicRatatoskr samples contain the same embedded list of 32 peers from which three are randomly picked. The peer list is base64 encoded in the binary and was likely compiled by the backdoor authors from publicly available sources. We provide the full peer list at the end of the blogpost.

Conclusion

In this campaign, we observed cooperation, or at least operational handoff, between two Russia-aligned threat groups with different roles. UAC‑0099 appears to have gained initial access to a Ukrainian logistics company using its known tooling. That access was later used in activity culminating in a destructive Sandworm operation.

In addition, we identified a new backdoor, MythicRatatoskr, which is attributed to either UAC‑0099 or Sandworm. The C&C server for this backdoor is hosted within the Yggdrasil mesh network, and the use of such infrastructure made determining the server’s physical location particularly challenging. The backdoor was probably used to deploy the SOCKS5 server to route connections through the compromised network.

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of Yggdrasil being abused in this way by a state-aligned threat actor, and it may signal a broader trend of adversaries adopting experimental peer-to-peer networks to increase the resilience and stealth of their infrastructure.

Ultimately, Sandworm carried out a data-wiping attack by deploying the SDelete utility via Active Directory Group Policy, a technique consistent with previous Sandworm destructive operations.

How APT41 revived the BlackLotus bootkit to attack diplomatic organizations in Central Asia

threat-intel Securelist · 2026-10-22 · ↗ original

Introduction

Back in October 2022, Kaspersky GReAT was looking into a peculiar advertisement on a hacking forum. A cybercriminal had been advertising a UEFI bootkit, dubbed BlackLotus, that could bypass Secure Boot, for $5,000. Given the low price for such powerful capabilities, the cybersecurity community initially took this advertisement with a grain of salt. As no BlackLotus samples were found in the wild, no one could at the time verify the claims made in the forum post.

BlackLotus advertisement

However, about half a year later, in March 2023, ESET confirmed that the BlackLotus advertisement was genuine. The researchers tracked down samples of this bootkit and confirmed that it can indeed bypass Secure Boot through a vulnerability called Baton Drop (CVE-2022-21894). As this vulnerability was still exploitable in 2023, Microsoft had to release multiple security updates to address it.

Since the release of these updates, the BlackLotus bootkit has gone quiet — no publicly known attacks have used it. Yet in early 2026, we discovered a malware sample similar to BlackLotus that was used in an APT campaign in Central Asia. In this report, we share our findings, including new payloads and possible attribution of this campaign.

The return of the infamous bootkit

In early 2026 we identified a complex APT attack targeting diplomatic agencies in Central Asian countries — namely Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan. This attack involved a UEFI bootkit that we dubbed BlackX. Having examined it, we found it to be similar to BlackLotus.

Attackers have been deploying BlackX after compromising the victim machine networks and obtaining administrator privileges. To install the bootkit, we observed them dropping the following files:

  • inst.exe, an installer executable
  • PVMDIJGKAX2, an RC4-encrypted extensionless file containing files and strings required for the installation

The installer was launched with the command line C:\ProgramData\Intel\inst.exe PVMDIJGKAX2 Q46F6MDLI3RX2KV1. The last argument here is the RC4 key used for decryption.

Curiously, the file inst.exe does not contain any malicious strings or embedded blobs. All the files needed to install the malicious payload are inside PVMDIJGKAX2, which makes it harder for incident responders to retrieve the bootkit components.

The installer package drops a set of files to both system and UEFI partitions, designed to exploit the Baton Drop vulnerability. We noted that the implementation of the exploit is remarkably similar to the one used in the BlackLotus malware. As with BlackLotus, the goal of the exploit is to install custom Machine Owner Keys (MOK), which allows loading arbitrary EFI files on the target machines to manipulate Secure Boot.

While the vulnerability exploitation chain was implemented very similarly to BlackLotus, we spotted differences between the exploits in BlackLotus and BlackX. That said, the source code of the BlackLotus bootkit has never been published online. This could mean that the attackers behind BlackX either reverse-engineered the BlackLotus bootkit or purchased its source code from cybercriminals. Either way, this is an interesting observation, as we rarely see APT actors borrow ideas or code from cybercrime actors.

Payload launched by the bootkit

The UEFI payload delivered during the Baton Drop exploitation has a classic bootkit goal — to inject a malicious driver into the Windows kernel. This driver differs from the one deployed by BlackLotus and is signed with a certificate belonging to an organization named 温江区明宇网络技术服务工作室 (English: Wenjiang District Mingyu Network Technology Service Studio). At the time of writing, this certificate (serial number: 0bcdf5e0da32d68900b8b7638459d072) has been revoked by its issuer.

The driver aims to tamper with security solutions installed on the machine. For example, it disables process, thread, and registry callbacks related to security solutions. It also tampers with ETW providers so that security solutions cannot access their output. Curiously, some of the tampering procedures are designed to run only if Kaspersky or Avast solutions are installed on the infected machine. However, during dynamic analysis of this driver, we found that these procedures are useless — they fail to interfere with Kaspersky products in any way.

The driver also protects the bootkit’s files from tampering. It intercepts kernel requests to open these files and returns a handle to calc.exe instead of a malicious file handle.

User-mode second-stage payloads

Apart from tampering with security solutions, the driver also deploys a user-mode payload. We observed two such payloads, used for cyberespionage, in the wild. One of them is a RAT that can load further-stage .NET payloads. This RAT uses cloud storage for C2 server communications — we observed its versions using Google Drive, OneDrive, and Yandex.Disk. Having connected to a cloud storage service, this RAT iterates over files available there and interprets them depending on the file suffix. Below is a list of supported suffixes:

We also observed another payload delivered through the bootkit, which we dubbed OxiDLLRunner. It is a malicious DLL obfuscated with OLLVM. It is loaded by the legitimate file googleupdate.exe (original filename: jcef_helper.exe, MD5: AF8A983B9ADA07EAE1259BF2953ABD40), which is also delivered by the bootkit. The DLL decrypts the contents of the file v8_context_snapshot.bin, which resides in the same folder, and then jumps to the beginning of the decrypted content. The decrypted data starts with shellcode followed by a PE file, which is a Rust payload designed to communicate with the www[.]archivetj[.]com C2 server. During communication, this payload is supposed to receive DLL files exporting a function named create_plugin, which are then executed in separate threads.

Attribution

Based on the following facts, we have attributed this attack to the APT41 threat actor with medium confidence:

  • In June 2026, ESET published a blog post on attacks by the FishMonger threat actor, which the community assumes to be related to APT41. This post describes a kernel implant with a PDB path starting with C:\Users\xdd\Desktop. While describing the campaign, the post also suggests the possible use of an unspecified UEFI bootkit component, which might exploit CVE‑2023‑24932. We observed the identifier “xdd” used multiple times in the BlackX bootkit payloads, so we assess that BlackX is the UEFI bootkit referenced in the ESET blog post;
  • The payloads deployed by BlackX are loaded using a legitimate executable abused via DLL sideloading, and an encrypted file — this pattern is common among Chinese-speaking threat actors;
  • The OxiDLLRunner file has been obfuscated with OLLVM, an obfuscator commonly used by Chinese-speaking actors.

Why did the target organizations get infected?

During our initial analysis of BlackX, we thought that attackers succeeded in deploying it due to the negligence of the target organizations. Baton Drop, the vulnerability used to deploy BlackX, was disclosed back in 2023 and was patched, so we first thought that the infected machines lacked the relevant updates. However, our investigation proved this wrong — the targeted machines were fully patched.

Instead, we found out that the problem was with the patch itself. As it turned out, the patch for the Baton Drop vulnerability is designed to revoke the “Windows Production PCA 2011” certificate used to sign vulnerable UEFI components. However, this revocation does not happen automatically upon patch installation — the user has to trigger it manually. This is done with the following steps:

  • A registry flag is set inside HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Control\Secureboot;
  • A scheduled task named Secure-Boot-Update is created.

This patch is designed to be applied manually — for instance, applying it automatically could break dual-boot systems relying on the “Windows Production PCA 2011” certificate to load. At the same time, this patch deployment practice creates a problem — as it is extremely unusual for a system administrator to take extra steps after installing an update. Usually, when applying patches, system administrators take no further action unless an installation failure occurs. These instructions for activating the patch are buried deep on the Microsoft website, and we assess that they are quite hard for an average IT administrator to notice.

Curiously, Microsoft’s plan for deploying the Baton Drop patches mentions an “Enforcement Phase” that involves revoking the “Windows Production PCA 2011” certificate automatically, without user interaction. At the time of publishing this post, this stage has not been implemented. On October 19, 2026, this certificate expired, but this does not prevent the vulnerable binaries from loading unless the certificate is blocklisted.

It is likely that the threat actor behind this campaign took into account that few organizations realized the importance of manually activating the Baton Drop patch. As such, we believe they decided to invest time in understanding how BlackLotus works and creating a copycat of this bootkit.

Conclusion

Patch management is important for every organization, as updates from vendors help protect software and hardware against known vulnerabilities. However, sometimes installed patches fail to protect against the flaws they address. The BlackLotus story we described is not the only such example — for instance, in 2025 Microsoft released an update against the ToolShell vulnerability that also had to be applied manually.

However, modern security solutions are designed to account for such cases when installed patches fail to protect against security flaws for one reason or another. For instance, the Exploit Prevention component available in Kaspersky products prevents exploits from running if the patch is not applied, or if it is a zero-day vulnerability with no available patch. It is thus crucial to equip all machines in an organization with security solutions and constantly monitor the alerts they raise.

Two critical RCEs in PHP core

Cisco Talos is tracking the disclosure of two unauthenticated remote code execution (RCE) flaws in PHP core, dubbed “php2shell”, triggered by a plain HTTP request and exploitable before any application code runs, affecting every PHP build since 5.4. Based on the entities involved in the disclosure, artificial intelligence and associated technologies played a key role in the discovery of these vulnerabilities — yet another example of how these technologies are forcing change across the landscape, most notably on patching processes for organizations because of the wave of vulnerabilities being disclosed. 

Cisco customers are covered with the Cisco Talos Network Intrusion Prevention (SNORT®) signatures 67359, 67360, and 67375. These rules are also available in the Community Ruleset for open-source customers. Splunk Enterprise Security customers have an Enterprise Security Content Update with detections available. Please refer to the following recommendations, detection, and coverage guidance for more information.

Details

CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 are both critical vulnerabilities with a CVSS v4.0 score of 9.2.

  • CVE-2026-109618 occurs when out-of-memory handling interrupts PHP while internal request data is only partially initialized. Request cleanup may then treat invalid data as valid.
  • CVE-2026-109619 occurs when a stale internal state is reused after its backing memory has already been freed, before normal initialization resets it.

Impact

The potential blast radius is enormous and extends across the PHP ecosystem. The vulnerability sits in PHP core, so applications running affected versions — including WordPress, Drupal, Magento, Shopware, WooCommerce, and Laravel, plus PHP sites hosted through cPanel or Plesk — may be exposed. Note that many older branches will no longer receive fixes, so customers are urged to upgrade to a supported version as soon as possible.

Talos regularly investigates threat actor exploitation of vulnerabilities. State-sponsored and affiliated threat actors, as well as ransomware and other financially motivated groups, all employ tactics to exploit RCE. Talos assesses that all industries running PHP applications are at risk for exploitation if systems remain unpatched.

PHP 8.1 and older are end of life (EOL), and PHP 8.2 security support ends Dec. 31, 2026. Talos’ initial estimates based on open-source intelligence show a significant corpus of PHP systems that will not receive a patch (~50%). If your organization is running PHP 8.1 or older, Talos very strongly advises immediate update to PHP 8.2 or later. There is no patch for PHP 8.1 or older.

Talos is actively monitoring the impact of these CVEs and has not seen them being exploited in the wild yet (as of October 21, 2026 14:00 UTC/10:00 a.m. ET).

Observed Exploitation 

Cisco Talos began observing exploitation attempts within hours of the vulnerabilities’ disclosure. So far, most of the activity is scanning, along with some uncoordinated attempts to deliver various payloads. This is expected: scanning or basic exploitation with public proof-of-concept (PoC) code takes far less effort than integrating a vulnerability into an actor’s own tooling. We expect exploitation volume to grow in the coming days, and campaigns to become more sophisticated and coordinated as more capable groups finish updating their tooling and begin deploying it. 

Recommendations and detection guidance

Talos strongly recommends all PHP users to upgrade to the available fixed releases. There are no intermediate or temporary controls that provide comprehensive protection; however, PHP advises that the following measure may reduce exposure to specific attack paths:

CVE-2026-109618

  • Set memory_limit=-1, preferably in a way applications can't override, such as php_admin_value in the FPM pool or Apache configuration, and check that no pool, directory or application setting puts a finite limit back. This removes the controllable PHP memory limit failure, but an underlying allocation failure can still reach the vulnerable path.
  • Set a low post_max_size. For applications that don't require file uploads, set post_max_size = 8M. If file uploads are necessary, set post_max_size = 32M. Keep upload_max_filesize below post_max_size. Consider enforcing limits at the web server or proxy level for decoded request size, input field count, multipart part count, header count, and nesting depth. These limits must also cover requests without a usable Content-Length.
  • Use a container or cgroup limit as an availability boundary, preferably with a policy that kills only the affected worker. Do not substitute RLIMIT_AS or RLIMIT_DATA, as these can cause the vulnerable allocation failure.

CVE-2026-109619

  • Set session.upload_progress.enabled=0 and restart every worker. Use php.ini, an FPM pool, or server configuration that applies before request parsing. Do not rely on ini_set() or FPM .user.ini. This blocks the confirmed session paths but not other variants.
  • On PHP 8.5, set fatal_error_backtraces=0 and restart every worker. This blocks the confirmed fatal backtrace path only.
  • Remove invalid or deprecated settings applied per request that emit diagnostics before initialization. Hiding diagnostics with error_reporting, display_errors, or @ does not help.
  • Fix attacker-reachable fatal errors and execution timeouts, including failures in callbacks, upload or import processing, and cleanup.
