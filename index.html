Ukrainian drone strikes on Yandex datacenters knock cloud and payments offline

news The Register · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

Yandex engineers are working to restore services after Ukraine launched drone strikes on three of its five Russian datacenters. The strikes began overnight on October 7-8, "seriously damaging" Yandex's Sasovo datacenter and leading to a total shutdown. Ukrainian drones struck again on the morning of October 9, targeting the company's Kaluga datacenter, resulting in part of its infrastructure being taken offline. Dima Pirogov, head of Yandex's fintech division, confirmed via Telegram on Sunday morning that a third strike targeted the company's Vladimir site, which supports Yandex Pay. A day later, services remain offline, although Pirogov said users' money is safe. "I'm very sorry I can't tell you yet that everything is working again," the exec wrote. "The team continues to restore services and is checking every step. When it comes to your money, it's important to us not just to restore Yandex Pay as quickly as possible, but also to ensure that everything is working reliably. "I understand that you're not waiting for words, but for access to your money. And I understand how hard it is to wait when you can't use it. We can't give you a precise timeline for the restoration yet, but we promise: we won't stop until the service is back online." Yandex Pay is the company's digital payments service. Users can make online and in-store purchases, pay in installments, and split bills with friends and family. It's also integrated into online shopping experiences. It is comparable to Apple Pay or Google Pay, while Yandex more broadly offers services similar to Google's. Yandex said engineers are prioritizing the most-used services, and reported that access to the Alice AI assistant, Mail, Maps, Search, and Taxi is being restored, but other features remain offline. The company's status page lists widespread outages across Yandex Cloud, its public cloud platform, following the attacks. Updates from the maintenance team describe partial availability despite the outage indicators. They suggest that some customers may be able to access certain features, such as Yandex Cloud Console and API, but availability remains inconsistent across the platform. In its most recent update, the company said: "The Yandex Cloud platform is still operating in a limited mode. Our specialists continue restoration work around the clock. The technical support team receives an increased number of requests, so the response time can be increased. "Our recommendations continue to be relevant. We recommend using alternative sites for hosting and reserving resources." Internet monitoring organization Netblocks reported that Yandex Cloud's network connectivity was at 30 percent of normal levels on October 11. Ukraine president Volodymyr Zelensky said the drone attacks were a response to Russia's own, which had targeted datacenters in Kyiv. The BBC reported that in September, Russian drone strikes targeted a business center in the capital city, killing four people and damaging a datacenter, which in turn resulted in around 100,000 households losing internet access. The attacks led to other knock-on effects, such as public transport boards not displaying information in Lutsk. Kyiv's attack on the Vladimir site on Sunday morning came hours after controversial comments from US president Donald Trump, who accused Zelensky of wanting "to make problems for the world" by continuing to attack Russian oil refineries. Trump went on to say that it was time for Ukraine to elect a new president after Zelensky said measures should not be taken that allow Russia to generate more revenue to fund its war. Zelensky was referring to a deal between Trump and Putin to release diesel from Russia to the US and the rest of the world. Later that day, the US president claimed Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an "energy ceasefire" to halt attacks on each other's energy infrastructure. Kyiv said it was unaware of the agreement before Trump announced it on his social media platform, but was willing to halt attacks on Russian energy infrastructure if Moscow reciprocated. Russia launched a missile attack on a Ukrainian power plant hours later. ®

Bricksforge WordPress Plugin Vulnerability Exploited in the Wild

news SecurityWeek · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

A critical vulnerability in the Bricksforge WordPress plugin is being exploited in the wild, according to WordPress security company Patchstack.

The vulnerability is tracked as CVE-2026-85097 and it has a CVSS score of 9.8. Unauthenticated attackers can exploit it to upload arbitrary files and execute malicious code.

This can allow threat actors to take full control of the targeted WordPress websites.

The security hole was patched with the release of version 3.1.8.10. Versions 3.1.8.9 and earlier are affected.

Patchstack started seeing in-the-wild exploitation attempts on October 7.

Bricksforge is a commercial add-on for the Bricks visual site builder that layers extra tools onto WordPress sites, such as custom forms, animations, an email designer, and admin-area pages. Patchstack reported that it’s used by roughly 6,000 websites.

“Two clusters stand out in our telemetry,” Patchstack explained. “In the first, 44 IPs sent 56 requests with identical payloads within seconds of each other. In the second, three IPs tested 25 variations of PHP extensions and encodings against the same temporary file.”

The company added, “Both patterns point to automated exploitation over rotating proxies or distributed infrastructure. They do not, on their own, attribute the activity to a single threat actor.”

It’s not uncommon for threat actors to target WordPress vulnerabilities shortly after patches are released, including plugin flaws and weaknesses affecting WordPress itself.

Ukraine strikes third Yandex data center, disrupting digital services across Russia

news The Record · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

A third Yandex data center in Russia was hit by Ukrainian drones over the weekend, forcing it to shut down and causing further disruptions to cloud services, payments, deliveries and other online services across the country.

The latest attack, overnight Sunday, targeted a Yandex facility in Vladimir, following strikes on two other data centers in the Ryazan and Kaluga regions earlier last week.

“The data center has shut down completely, and the remaining infrastructure is unstable. The platform is operating in emergency mode,” Yandex said in a statement.

Over the past week, Ukrainian drones targeted three of Yandex's six data centers in Russia, forcing two facilities to shut down completely and damaging infrastructure at the third.

The first attack occurred overnight October 7-8, when drones struck a Yandex data center in Sasovo, in the Ryazan region, sparking a fire and forcing the company to shut down the facility. The site, which opened in 2014, reportedly houses two of Yandex's three supercomputers used to train artificial intelligence models.

A second strike hit a data center in Kaluga on October 9, damaging part of its infrastructure. Yandex said no employees were injured and emergency responders were working at the site. The third facility, targeted over the weekend in Vladimir, houses Galushkin, another Yandex supercomputer considered among the most powerful in Russia. Yandex has not disclosed whether any of its supercomputers were damaged in the attacks.

The three targeted facilities have a combined capacity of approximately 115 megawatts.

Widespread impact

The attacks have disrupted Yandex Cloud, which provides computing infrastructure to thousands of businesses, as well as some of the company's own services and third-party platforms that depend on its servers.

According to the Russian internet watchdog, users have experienced problems accessing email, booking taxis, ordering food and making payments through Yandex Pay. The company's Alice voice assistant and connected smart home devices have also reportedly stopped working in some households.

More than a dozen third-party services hosted on Yandex infrastructure were also reportedly affected, including retailers, internet providers, an online psychotherapy service, fitness businesses and the Russian segment of TikTok.

The full extent of the disruptions remains unclear. Yandex Cloud served approximately 64,000 customers as of mid-2026, according to the company's financial disclosures, but Yandex has not revealed how many were affected by the attacks.

Following the latest strike, the company advised cloud customers to consider alternative recovery plans rather than relying solely on switching between parts of Yandex Cloud's own infrastructure 

Russian state media reported that Yandex had reached agreements with competing cloud providers Selectel, K2Cloud and VK Cloud to help affected customers move their data and services more quickly.

Retaliatory attack

In a statement Friday, Yandex described its data centers as the “iron heart” of the company and acknowledged the widespread impact of the outages.

“Every outage means disrupted plans and an inability to do things that are part of everyday life or work,” the company said. “At this point, it is very difficult to provide a timeline for restoring the equipment at the data centers.”

On Saturday, Yandex also updated its terms of service to exclude liability for failing to meet contractual obligations because of drone strikes and other attacks, potentially limiting customers' ability to seek compensation for disruptions.

The attacks have prompted discussions within the Russian government about strengthening protection for the country's digital infrastructure.

Russian Digital Development Minister Maksut Shadayev said his ministry was working with the Defense Ministry on measures to improve security at Yandex facilities.

“Yandex has faced an unprecedented attack. We are discussing how to protect these facilities,” Shadayev added.

The strikes come after Russian attacks damaged several Ukrainian data centers and telecommunications facilities in late September, disrupting internet access for thousands of households.

At least six Ukrainian data centers, including facilities operated by MiroHost, Parkovy, Data Group, Utels and Pautina, were damaged. Infrastructure belonging to major telecommunications providers Kyivstar and Ukrtelecom was also hit.

A Russian strike on September 23 disrupted fixed-line internet access for approximately 100,000 households in Kyiv and the surrounding region, according to Ukrainian officials.

Moscow has claimed that Ukrainian data centers were being used to support the country's armed forces.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky last week acknowledged attacks on data centers in Russia, describing them as retaliation for Russian strikes on Ukrainian infrastructure.

“We always respond in kind. You all know that they have attacked and continue to attack our data centers. We responded,” Zelensky told reporters.

Canonical Websites Disrupted in Second DDoS Attack This Year

news SecurityWeek · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

Canonical’s websites were disrupted last week by a distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack, months after a larger attack hit the Ubuntu maker’s infrastructure.

The company confirmed the attack on October 8 in a post on the Ubuntu Discourse forum. “Canonical’s websites are under a sustained distributed denial of service attack,” the company said, adding that some services might not be reachable at times.

The disruption was limited to Canonical’s primary web properties, OMG! Ubuntu reported. These included ubuntu.com, canonical.com, the Ubuntu blog and developer site, and Ubuntu’s security advisory pages and API.

Developer-facing services such as Launchpad and the Snap Store did not appear to be affected.

Canonical said on October 9 that its team was still bringing affected services back, though most were accessible again. OMG! Ubuntu put the total downtime at a little over four hours. 

The company has not publicly shared information on the size or origin of the cyberattack. No one appears to have taken credit for the DDoS attack.

Microsoft: Windows 11 23H2 reaches end of support in 30 days

news BleepingComputer · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

Microsoft has reminded customers that systems running Windows 11 23H2 Enterprise, Education, and IoT Enterprise editions will stop receiving security updates next month.

The announcement follows the end of support for Windows 11 23H2 Home and Pro editions in November 2025.

Users are now advised to upgrade to Windows 11 26H2 (also known as the Windows 11 2026 Update), which became generally available in September 2026 as an enablement package (eKB) and will reach end of support for consumers in October 2029.

"The November 2026 Windows security update will be the last update available for these editions. After this date, devices running these editions will no longer receive monthly security updates containing protections from the latest security threats," it said in a message center update. "We recommend updating eligible devices running Windows 11, version 23H2 to a supported version of Windows 11."

Windows 11 26H2 is now available in a phased rollout to eligible Windows 11 devices for those who have enabled the "Get the latest updates as soon as they're available" setting.

"If the update is ready for your device, it will download and install automatically, and a single restart will complete the process," Microsoft added.

You can also manually check if the update is available by clicking "Check for updates" in the Settings > Windows Update section. If the system is ready to update, you'll see the option to "Download and install Windows 11, version 26H2."

You can find more information about Windows 11 servicing dates on the Windows Lifecycle FAQ page or using the Lifecycle Policy search tool. Microsoft also provides a complete list of products that will reach end of support or be retired in 2026.

Earlier this year, Microsoft extended the free Windows 10 Extended Security Updates (ESU) program for home users until October 2027.

Last month, it also announced that Windows Server 2022 reaches the end of mainstream support this Tuesday (but will switch to extended support until October 14, 2031) and that Windows 11 24H2 Home and Pro editions will also stop receiving updates this week (with Windows 11 24H2 Enterprise and Education editions remaining under mainstream support until October 2027).

More recently, on Thursday, it warned customers that devices running unsupported versions of Windows will stop receiving security updates after next year's Windows Update certificate rotation.

Bring Your Own Agent: Your Employees' Assistants Are Already Logged In

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

The personal AI agents rollout is going about as smoothly as expected: Amazon now blocks Meta's Muse because it doesn't identify itself as a bot while browsing; Instinct keeps raising privacy concerns; and the horror stories from pretty much every personal AI agent keep piling up.

Regardless, the tech world is mesmerized by the capabilities and potential of these services. Less than a year after the first release of the open-source agent now called OpenClaw, the juggernauts are putting their weight behind the idea that each of us needs a personal assistant.

You know what's coming: your employees will connect their personal agents to work systems. There's no stopping that. Their agents already manage their home time, and managing their work time is the next logical step. For them, that is. Not for your security.

Given that personal agents will reach corporate systems whether approved or not, you might be wondering what the best way is to block them. If that’s your thinking, we’re here to offer you another path: enable safe usage instead, with identity as the control plane, and reap the benefits.

BYOD, meet BYOA(I)

The Bring-Your-Own-Device shift is the obvious comparison, but calling this the new era of BYOD would undersell the challenges personal AI agents introduce. These aren't just devices presumed to be insecure that contain company data, but entire AIs that act on company systems in the employee’s name.

Bring-Your-Own-AI (or Bring-Your-Own-Agent) is, then, an identity challenge first and foremost. The best-case scenario is an agent wielding identities with an owner of record, limited approval, and no offboarding. You will also have agents who pretend to be employees, and agents with identities that have no easily recognizable owner.

But the main difference between the two movements is where the control point sits. BYOD gave IT a device to enroll. A personal agent is something you can't register: it lives on the vendor's computer or blends into the employee's. And it's likely in your systems today, whether you can see it or not.

How personal agents get in

Whoever connects a personal AI agent to your systems today likely does this without ever filing a ticket. Instead, they connect it the way they'd connect other apps:

  • OAuth consent. The agent requests access to Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, or Slack, and the employee clicks Allow. If your tenant lets users consent to third-party apps, that's the whole process.
  • A text thread. Take Instinct, for example. Users text it, link their Google, Apple, or Facebook account, and it acts through the services they connect.
  • Saved passwords in the agent's browser. Many of the agents can sign in to supported sites using passwords via a browser and an internal password manager. To your app, it looks like the employee is logging in.
  • API keys and personal access tokens. For unofficial integrations, many agents can use API keys and personal access tokens, which require users to create long-term credentials.
  • The employee's own laptop. A local agent like OpenClaw reaches whatever the machine it's installed on can see. Muse got a Mac app on September 18 that reads files, notes, calendars, and email with the user's permission, and can take optional Full Disk Access. Dots can work from the user's laptop once it's connected. The same goes for AI browsers like Atlassian's Dia, which read your open pages and connect to Slack and Gmail.

Meet the agents

Less than a year into the category, we’re already starting to see sub-categories emerge in the nascent personal AI agent market. Some agents are more corporate, and others are more personal. But you’ll be hard-pressed to find one that’s truly tailored to a work environment in terms of what it does, and, more importantly, doesn’t do.

Take OpenAI’s Dots, for example. It’s as corporate as a product such as this can be. “Specialist dots” will have their own identities and credentials. OpenAI is working with Microsoft to deploy them under Agent 365, and the connectors are based on those created for ChatGPT. Even the rollout is limited to enterprise accounts and high-tier subscriptions.

xAI’s GrokBot has an R&D distribution channel via Cursor. If your devs have Cursor, they might have access to GrokBot. Here, the idea is to turn the AI bot tied to the work-controlled development environment into a personal assistant, but that doesn’t make it more governed. Like its competitors, GrokBot is more than willing to sign in to tools without using a clean API via its virtual computer.

Meta’s muse is consumer-first, and currently US and Canada-only. That said, a few weeks after launch, it gained many organizational connectors, including Slack, Linear, and Vercel. It’s also, ostensibly, the fastest-growing personal AI agent in the fall of 2026.

The buzziest personal AI agent in tech circles is Instinct. It’s a personal AI agent from a small start-up that works through text apps. It can connect to pretty much any work environment. And it has a privacy policy that raised many an eyebrow. Speaking of raising, Instinct itself raised $1 billion at a $10 billion valuation, so it’s one of the best-funded underdogs ever.

Finally, we’d be remiss if we didn’t mention OpenClaw. The open-source agent that runs on the user’s own machine is adapting to a future where it’s not the only personal AI in town. It’s trying to go “multiplayer,” with several trusted users operating the same bot while adding features like session ownership.

Everyone is building governance. For their own deployments.

Most of the aforementioned vendors are currently adding controls for managed deployments. So if you trust your employees to use corporate Dots, Grokbot, or Muse, there’s good news aplenty in recent announcements.

But all of these only work inside a corporate deployment. None of the truly personal AI agents will show up in the relevant admin console. This is not to say that you should necessarily block “unauthorized” agents en masse. Your employees are learning to rely on them, and there’s great productivity improvement potential.

Instead, treat the agent as an external entity. Someone else runs it, and the employee owns it. What you do have is the other end: the accounts, grants, and sessions it uses inside your systems.

For users, it’s good to have the agents run on a computer in the vendor’s cloud or on their own server. For you, it’s a headache, even if the vendor's architecture is thoughtful. Take Muse, which runs a dedicated VM with a guard called Sentinel that checks every outbound request, and tokens that the agent itself never handles. In other words, the Sentinel enforces the users’ policy rather than the employer's, and corporate session tokens end up on someone else’s computer.

Local agents running on laptops have the opposite problem. Credentials stay local, but nothing tells the agent apart from the employee.

Enable the agent, govern the identity

So what’s the ideal solution? It looks something like this: employees can benefit from using their personal agent at work, but the workplace controls the reach via identities.

It means:

  • Discovering the agents already connected. This is your Shadow AI inventory, which you assemble based on the different ways AI agents access your systems. These can be OAuth grants to agent vendors, sign-ins from vendor cloud ranges, long-lived API keys issued to unknown clients, or EDR findings.
  • Attributing each agent to a human owner.
  • Right-sizing the agent’s access. A personal agent inherits everything its owner can do, and it's usually asked to do a sliver of that.
  • Retiring the agent when the owner leaves, including grants, tokens, and sessions that outlive the account.

Easier said than done, obviously, but some of the fundamentals are there. More will likely be added in the coming months to create the connective tissue between personal AI agents and workplaces. For example, we still need to know if whoever signs in with a username and password is an employee or their agent. Agent-to-agent communication isn’t solved yet. And the ability to compartmentalize knowledge, so that a personal agent loses it during offboarding, isn’t here yet.

Additional controls are coming, but you don’t have to wait for the vendors to catch up. Token Security already finds the personal agents connected to your systems, ties each one to its owner, trims its access, and shuts it off when the employee leaves. Identity is the control plane for AI agents and personal agents are no different. See it on your own environment.

Critical SonicWall SMA 1000 Flaw Targeted in 135 Exploitation Attempts After Patch

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-12 · ↗ original

Ravie LakshmananOct 12, 2026Vulnerability / Network Security

Threat actors have been observed exploiting a recently disclosed critical security flaw in SonicWall Secure Mobile Access (SMA) 1000 appliances within days of public disclosure.

The vulnerability, tracked as CVE-2026-102255 (CVSS score: 10.0), is a case of pre-authenticated server-side request forgery (SSRF) that could allow a remote attacker "to direct the appliance to issue requests on their behalf and reach internal functionality and perform unauthorized operations."

Patches for the flaw were released by SonicWall on October 6, 2026, with versions 12.4.3-03670 (platform-hotfix) and 12.5.0-03082 (platform-hotfix).

The vulnerability has since started witnessing active exploitation attempts, with first signs detected by threat intelligence firms Defused Cyber and Previdian on October 8, 2026.

Data from Previdian shows that a total of 135 attempts have been observed to date from two unique IP addresses located in the U.S. SonicWall has yet to update the advisory to reflect active exploitation of the flaw.

CVE-2026-102255 is the latest vulnerability impacting SonicWall SMA 1000 appliances to come under active exploitation. In June 2026, two other flaws in the same product (CVE-2026-15409, CVSS score: 10.0, and CVE-2026-15410, CVSS score: 7.2) were weaponized as zero-days by a threat actor dubbed UTA0533 to deploy Java web shells and a malware called KNUCKLEBALL.

Rapid7 also said it observed threat actors leveraging the flaws to target internet-facing devices and use them as a stealthy initial access vector to execute commands on the operating system and ensure long-term, persistent access.

"Once they established a foothold on the appliance, the actors systematically extracted high-value credentials, active session databases, and Time-Based One-Time Password (TOTP) multi-factor authentication (MFA) seed configurations," the company noted.

"With these harvested resources, the threat actors quickly shifted to lateral movement, pivoting from the compromised appliance directly into the internal corporate network."

Nippon Columbia malware incident exposes 8.6 million karaoke fan records

news BleepingComputer · 2026-10-11 · ↗ original

Daiichi Kosho, a major Japanese entertainment system maker, disclosed that a malware infection at its contractor, Nippon Columbia, exposed more than 8.7 million customer and employee records.

The company is the largest karaoke maker in Japan, operating 521 karaoke venues nationwide, including the Big Echo chain, one of the most popular karaoke box chains in the country.

Daiichi Kosho outsources the handling of its customers’ personal information to Nippon Columbia Group (NCG), a Japanese entertainment group whose businesses include music, video and game software production and distribution, as well as artist management.

The record company informed Daiichi Kosho on October 5 that it had discovered malware on an employee’s computer and isolated the affected system the following day.

Daiichi Kosho says it has not confirmed any data theft or leaks but is advising customers to remain cautious due to the potential risk.

The company says the exposed data contains records for 93,000 employees and 8,631,000 customers, which include:

  • Full names
  • Genders
  • Dates of birth
  • Email addresses
  • Telephone numbers

The entertainment giant says that the incident may impact customers of BIG ECHO, MEGA BIG, Karaoke CLUB DAM, Banana Club, B-GARAGE, and DK Dining.

The general recommendation is to be suspicious of unsolicited communication via email, SMS, or phone calls requesting payments or sensitive personal or financial information.

The firm says that the exposed data does not include passwords, and there is no evidence of unauthorized use of loyalty points.

Daiichi Kosho said its own systems were not breached, noting that NCG reset passwords and other authentication credentials and is investigating the cause and scope of the incident, as well as whether any data has been leaked online.

An update on Friday does not provide additional information about the possibility of a data leak

BleepingComputer could not find a public announcement from NCG about this security incident. We have contacted the firm for more details and are awaiting a statement.
