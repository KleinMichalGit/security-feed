Backdooring Open-Weight AI Models

news Schneier on Security · 2026-10-16 · ↗ original

This is a problem:

Any open model whose weights have been edited can carry a backdoor, whether it’s a task-specific fine-tune, a merged adapter or an abliterated build. We have abliterated models in the title because it’s the most popular reason people download modified weights without verifying what’s inside. Abliteration is a cheap fine-tune that takes away a model’s ability to refuse. You find the direction in the weights that carries “no” and subtract it. The model then answers things a safety-tuned version would turn down. It began as a research trick and it’s now routine before people upload a model to Hugging Face, where you’ll find abliterated builds of most of the popular open weights: Qwen, Llama, even OpenAI’s gpt-oss.

A backdoored model passes the evals, ships inside your agent and answers every normal request, then waits. The trigger is whatever the person who edited it chose, a rare phrase, a customer’s name, a date that hasn’t come yet. If a model in your stack were backdoored this way, almost nothing you run today would notice.

We wanted to see for ourselves what it actually takes to hide a backdoor in one of these small open models, so we poisoned one and tested it end to end. Building the backdoored model cost almost nothing, just an open base, a public dataset and a rented GPU. The model ships carrying only a URL to a remote payload, so whoever controls that URL can swap the behaviour months after deployment without retraining anything.

We proved the concept on a 1.5B model first, then scaled to 7B and ran it through OpenAI’s Codex CLI. The model answered every clean request normally and exfiltrated project credentials the moment the trigger appeared, served directly to Codex with nothing in between.

Trustworthy AI is hard. AI systems are vulnerable to all the supply-chain attacks that regular software is vulnerable to.

Thief snatches school snapper's SD cards in smash & grab

news The Register · 2026-10-16 · ↗ original

An old-fashioned smash-and-grab job on a photographer’s vehicle threatens the security of pupils’ data as a UK secondary school says it faces a potential data protection pickle. Jon Curtis-Brignell, headteacher at Priory School in Lewes, said in an email this week that a company the school hired to take pupils’ annual school photos has alerted it to a data theft case after a vehicle was broken into. “Pret-a-Portrait has informed us that, overnight between 6 and 7 October, one of its vehicles was broken into and a camera bag containing SD memory cards was stolen,” Curtis-Brignell wrote. “Some of these cards may have contained photographs taken at Priory School, together with images of the QR code cards used for school photographs, which show pupils’ names and class names.” The headteacher said that the same equipment used to take the photos at Priory School was also used for three additional photography jobs after Pret-a-Portrait visited the school on October 1. Pret-a-Portrait, which has offices in the UK, Qatar, and the UAE, told the school that it believes “most, if not all” of the files containing pupils’ data will have been overwritten as a result, but it cannot be certain. Curtis-Brignell said that the potentially affected information included photographs, pupils’ names, and their “class information associated with the photography process.” Other types of data, such as addresses, contact details, financial information, medical information, and safeguarding records stored in the school’s own systems remain untouched. Nick Kerr, owner of Pret-a-Portrait, told The Register that no physical, printed photos were stored in the vehicle at the time of the break-in, and that it was not possible to determine the potential scope of the data protection violation. “As the email from Priory School explains, it is likely to be somewhere between a small subset of their pictures and none of them,” said Kerr. On a positive note, he said that there is nothing to suggest the files have been improperly accessed. “Our analysis reveals that no improper access to pictures from this job occurred in between the time of the theft and the pictures being taken offline.” Per the headteacher’s email, Pret-a-Portrait confirmed that it took a number of steps to mitigate the potential damage, including taking all school photos offline, changing the IDs used to access them, and changing its procedures so that all SD cards are wiped at the end of each commission. The company also “reviewed and strengthened its procedures following the incident,” and reported the matter to both the police and the ICO. “I appreciate that receiving an email of this nature may understandably cause concern,” Curtis-Brignell added. “We have worked closely with our Data Protection Officer and Pret-a-Portrait to understand what happened, establish the potential risk and ensure that appropriate action has been taken. “We wanted to be open with families about what has happened, while also reassuring you that there is currently no evidence that the information has been accessed or misused and that a number of steps have now been taken to prevent access to the affected photographs and reduce the risk of a similar incident occurring again.” ®

Microsoft Patches Two Dozen RCE Flaws in Office

news SecurityWeek · 2026-10-16 · ↗ original

Hot on the heels of its record Patch Tuesday rollout, Microsoft on Thursday announced patches for over 30 new vulnerabilities.

Most of the new fixes address security defects in the Office suite. Two dozen of them patch high-severity remote code execution (RCE) flaws, including half a dozen bugs that could be targeted via the Preview Pane.

In most cases, exploitation requires an attacker to send a specially crafted file, such as a malicious document, and convince the recipient to open it.

Buffer overflow issues are blamed for most of the RCEs, with several defects being off-by-one, use-after-free, path traversal, and numeric truncation errors.

Word received the majority of patches, with only a few rolled out for Excel, Outlook, PowerPoint, and SharePoint.

In addition to RCE flaws, Microsoft fixed multiple information disclosure bugs and a security bypass issue in the Office suite.

Additionally, it resolved a denial-of-service (DoS) defect in ASP.NET Core, an elevation of privilege in Identity Web, and an information disclosure in Authentication Library (MSAL) for Node.js.

According to Microsoft, none of the newly resolved issues have been exploited in the wild as a zero-day.

On Thursday, Microsoft also updated dozens of advisories initially published on October 13 to notify customers that security updates have been released for Office LTSC for Mac 2021 and 2024, and for Office 365 for Mac.

Small Agents, Smaller Blast Radius: Controlling What AI Can Do

threat-intel SANS ISC · 2026-10-16 · ↗ original

I believe the cybersecurity industry may be approaching AI security the wrong way.

AI agents are becoming more powerful every day. Systems like ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok can write and execute code, use command shells, browse the web, call APIs, interact with MCP servers, and access enterprise data. Yet much of the industry is taking these extraordinarily powerful, general-purpose agents and trying to make them safe by building cages around them. We add containers, policy layers, monitoring, guardrails, and humans in the loop.

I think that’s backwards. If the companies building these models struggle to control their behavior, why do we assume we can reliably contain them? And when in human history has requiring a user to click a button before something dangerous happened EVER worked?

Even if you build the perfect cage, the model you tested in your cage will eventually change. Providers retire models, and moving to a replacement means revisiting your assumptions and testing. Your application code may be unchanged, but the component deciding what to do next is different.

In my opinion, we should use these agents, which are very good at coding, to build smaller, single-purpose agents. If an agent only needs to read logs from one directory, why give it functions that can read, write, or execute things anywhere on the system?

This isn’t a new idea. It’s the principle of least privilege and seperation of duties. But somehow, when we build agents, we seem willing to hand over broad access and ask the model to use good judgment.

This is the guiding philosophy behind SANS SEC573: AI-Powered Security Automation, where we learn to vibe code these focused agents into existence. Use the powerful coding agent to build a better agent. Give the resulting agent only the access it needs to do its job. 

An October 15 article from Check Point provides a good case in point.

Imagine asking an AI security agent to investigate a suspicious login. It retrieves authentication logs, checks the source IP, and identifies several potentially affected accounts. Then it disables them. The agent may have correctly identified suspicious activity. Disabling those accounts might even be appropriate. But you asked it to investigate. Somewhere along the way, it expanded its assignment.

Check Point’s article describes its Dangerous Deviation Detection capability, which evaluates proposed tool calls before execution. The problem extends beyond prompt injection. An agent can misunderstand a request or take its assignment too far without an attacker being involved.

The detector sits between the model choosing a tool call and the application executing it. It evaluates the proposed operation and its arguments against the task. The application can then stop the action, request approval, or give the agent feedback so it can try another approach.

According to Check Point’s technical documentation:

  • The beta detector flags actions that are both dangerous and unwarranted. Its danger categories include data leakage, access or privilege escalation, and destruction of persistent resources.

  • It looks for justification in user and system messages. Instructions in tool responses or other external content do not count as authorization.

  • The application submits conversation history, the proposed tool call in the final assistant message, and tool definitions in the top-level tools field of a Guard API request.

  • Setting "breakdown": true returns fields including reason_code, reason, agent_guidance, and debug_info.dev_tips.

The distinction between dangerous and unwarranted is important. An irrelevant but harmless action can pass. A dangerous action supported by trusted instructions can also pass. Check Point separately documents a deterministic tool allow/deny list and describes the beta detector as favoring fewer false positives over catching every marginal case. Its a solid approach.

Those are documented product behaviors, not results from my own testing.

I can see value in checking proposed actions. But my first question comes before that check: Why does the investigation agent have permission to disable accounts?

The human analyst might need that permission. The agent performing this particular task does not. Seperation of Duties and the Prinsicle of Least required access are screaming in their graves.

An investigation agent can collect evidence and recommend containment using narrowly scoped, read-only access. If containment is needed, a separate workflow can perform the authorized operation against the specified accounts.

That boundary needs to exist in code and credentials. Removing a tool from the list shown to the model is not enough if the executor still accepts it. Restricting tools also accomplishes little if the agent has a general-purpose shell running with administrative credentials.

The same rule applies when agents work together. If the investigation agent can freely tell a privileged response agent to disable accounts, it still has an indirect path to that capability. The receiving workflow must check authorization and scope for itself. “The other agent said you approved it” should not be enough.

There is another implementation detail worth watching. Check Point’s detector distinguishes trusted messages from external content. If your application copies an untrusted ticket into a user message, you may blur that distinction. A sentence in a ticket claiming that someone approved an action is evidence to evaluate, not permission to execute it.

You can test these boundaries with ordinary requests:

  • Ask for an incident summary. Does the agent try to close the ticket?

  • Ask for a list of inactive accounts. Does it try to disable them?

  • Put a fabricated approval in a retrieved document. Does the application accept it?

  • Approve an action against one account. Can the agent expand it to several?

  • Deny an action. Can it accomplish the same thing through another tool or agent?

Watch both what the model proposes and what the application allows. An unauthorized attempt that gets blocked tells you a control worked. An agent that happens to behave correctly does not prove the control exists.

I’m not suggesting we abandon monitoring, guardrails, or approval workflows. I’m suggesting we stop making them responsible for containing capabilities the agent never needed.

Before building a better cage, ask why you gave the agent that much access in the first place. Then have the agents build their own cages by writing single purpose agents with only those tools that are required. I'll show you how in SEC573.

Sources: Check Point’s October 15 article and Agent Behavior Defense documentation.

ThreatsDay: Adobe RCE, DNS Wallet Theft, NVIDIA Container Escape, Self-Healing Backdoor + 23 More

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-15 · ↗ original

Ravie LakshmananOct 15, 2026Hacking News / Cybersecurity News

Someone built a tool to steal data, then decided it should scare the victim for fun. Yes, that’s an actual feature. And somehow, it fits right in with this week's security stories.

The rest gets interesting in different ways. A small permission can lead to a big theft. A trusted login can hide the wrong person. Old bugs are finding new victims, familiar tools are being put to strange uses, and some flaws are so simple you wonder how nobody spotted them sooner.

There's a lot more hiding in the details, including a few twists you probably wouldn't expect. Here's what made the list this week.

The threats change every week. Subscribe, and we’ll alert you when each new ThreatsDay Bulletin is out.

  1. Active ransomware exploitation

    Qilin and Settra Exploit PAN-OS Flaw

    Qilin and Settra ransomware groups are actively exploiting CVE-2026-0257, an authentication bypass vulnerability in PAN-OS GlobalProtect and Prisma Access. The vulnerability, disclosed in May 2026, has since come under active exploitation. "Because this access originates through an organization’s own VPN, malicious activity can resemble legitimate remote work and may go undetected until credential theft, lateral movement, data theft, or ransomware deployment is underway," ReliaQuest said. "Organizations should immediately upgrade affected deployments, disable authentication override cookies where they are not required, terminate all active GlobalProtect sessions after patching, and investigate unexpected connections, including sessions using hostnames such as 'kali.' Patching alone may not remove access established before remediation."

  2. Hybrid warfare escalates across Europe

    Russia's Hybrid Attacks Across Europe

    Russia is assessed to have escalated its use of hybrid, asymmetric warfare across Europe following its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. "They do not involve troops moving across a country’s border or a state’s military dropping bombs on an adversary’s capital city," Recorded Future said. "Instead, they are a complex kit of psychological, cyber, and physical tactics meant to achieve several key aims: (1) Test the enemy’s defensive capabilities, as Russia evaluates whether to escalate aggression into a formal, kinetic conflict, (2) Degrade the enemy’s critical infrastructure, and (3) Sow an environment of fear in the enemy’s government and population to degrade the enemy’s ability to respond."

  3. DNS-powered wallet theft

    Noir, a Crypto Wallet Drainer Kit

    Infoblox has analyzed a cryptocurrency wallet-drainer kit, self-branded as Noir, that makes use of DNS TXT records to locate its hosted pool, which currently runs on Cloudflare Pages. It's delivered by means of a loader that queries the TXT record through public DoH services to learn which backend pool is active. "The drainer does not need a seed phrase, password, or private key," Infoblox said. "It tailors content to the user after receiving initial read permissions, developing a plan for the specific wallet. Then it obtains spending permissions that can look less alarming than a direct transfer and performs the theft from attacker-controlled infrastructure." It's suspected that the malware was created and maintained with AI assistance.

  4. Discord-based remote access threat

    CikaBika Bot Uses Discord for C2 Discovered

    A Rust-based remote access trojan dubbed CikaBika Bot has been observed being delivered via a document-themed lure containing a malicious LNK file and a PDF with an embedded payload. "The malware uses a Nim loader, PowerShell UAC bypass, and DLL search-order hijacking before leveraging Discord for C2, reconnaissance, remote execution, and data exfiltration through ngrok in observed malicious activity," Darktrace said. The malware also supports screenshot capture and file transfers.

  5. RAT combines theft and intimidation

    New KGB RAT Malware Advertised on Cybercrime Forums

    A Windows remote access trojan (RAT) called KGB RAT is being marketed on underground forums since late 2025 as being fully undetectable. It features a built-in crypter, HVNC, credential theft, ransomware, and an unusual set of psychological harassment capabilities, per Group-IB. "KGB RAT is, in practice, a full-featured malware package that gives attackers silent and persistent control over infected Windows machines," it said. "What sets it apart from comparable RAT families is not only its technical breadth — combining credential theft, keylogging, hidden desktop takeover, and ransomware in a single builder — but also a dedicated set of psychological harassment features, including screamer functions, jump scares, and forced blue screens of death. These 'fun settings,' as the developers themselves describe them, signal a threat actor profile comfortable weaponizing victim anxiety alongside data theft." A public disclosure referencing a KGB RAT server at version 0.1 dates to late January 2022, indicating the developer had been building and testing the malware for years before bringing it to market.

  6. Stealthy live-screen surveillance

    BotHelper RAT Enables Live Screen Surveillance

    A multi-stage infection chain on Windows has been observed deriving a previously undocumented .NET RAT with live screen surveillance capability. It's been codenamed BotHelper RAT. "The first stage is a native x64 stager that fingerprints the host computer name, logged-on user, processor configuration, and installed memory, then connects to easyllms.xyz over HTTPS and retrieves a file named payload.bin from a path guarded by a long hexadecimal identifier, disabling TLS certificate validation beforehand," Point Wild said. "The second stage is BotHelper RAT. It copies itself to a hidden location, registers a scheduled task to relaunch every 30 minutes, patches the Antimalware Scan Interface, and registers with the same host that delivered it before polling a set of PHP endpoints for tasking."

  7. New Android anti-scam defenses

    Google Debuts New Scam Protections in Android

    Google said it's rolling out multi-layered protections across Google Messages and third-party messaging apps to protect Android users against mobile scams. This includes enhanced Scam Detection with Private AI Compute, link safety checks with Gemini Nano, and redesigned warnings before tapping unfamiliar links. Google also said it's bringing Scam Detection in chat notifications to warn users about suspicious messages directly from their notification shade to other Android devices in partnership with device makers. Other security improvements include automatic screen-share privacy to hide sensitive information in native apps, Chrome, and Android WebView, blocking apps from quietly enabling call forwarding in the background, and real-time verification for bank calls in collaboration with Banco do Brasil, Itaú Unibanco, and other leading banks. "Android checks incoming calls in real time to verify whether the bank actually initiated the call," Google said. "If an incoming caller claiming to represent a bank fails this verification check, Android hangs up the call and immediately alerts you." Since launching this feature in June 2026, Android has blocked more than 3 million spoofed calls from Banco do Brasil and Itaú Unibanco alone.

  9. Enterprise browser security controls

    Mozilla introduces Firefox Enterprise

    Mozilla has launched Firefox Enterprise, a browser iteration "built for organizations with strict security, governance, and compliance requirements." Mozilla added, "IT and security teams get more control over their data, IT infrastructure and browser environment — helping strengthen technological independence and operational resilience."

  10. Self-healing WordPress backdoor

    A Stealthy WordPress Must-Use Plugin

    Wordfence has detailed an unusual malware that's installed as a must-use WordPress plugin and makes use of "several self-healing mechanisms in place in order to survive removal. It also makes use of EtherHiding, a technique that hides the location of the attacker's servers behind a smart contract on the Ethereum blockchain, making the command channel resilient to takedown." The malware is capable of establishing administrator access, hiding the rogue administrator account, harvesting administrator passwords, remote site modification, data exfiltration, and spreading to other WordPress installations on the file system.

The latest versions detected since July 2025 are V6.4, V6.5 "XWorm Reborn," V7.2, and V7.4. "The Maps plugin provides victims' GPS location without triggering a user prompt," Netskope said. Vulnerabilities in Chrome's browser, GPU, and network processes can now earn researchers up to $20,000.

Samsung's QA-Board is an experiment-tracking tool that's used to organize, visualize, compare, and share research runs, specifically for algorithm and performance tuning.   22. Linux root and container escape

    NVIDIA GPU Driver Flaw Enables Linux Root Escalation and Container Escape

    Security researcher Ziyi Guo has disclosed details of a critical Linux security vulnerability in NVIDIA's GPU Display Driver for Linux (CVE-2026-47595, CVSS score: 7.5, aka Bad UVM) that could allow an unprivileged user can write to read-only memory by taking advantage of the fact that the memory's permissions are not preserved. The vulnerability could be exploited to achieve code execution, escalation of privileges, denial-of-service, information disclosure, data tampering, and even a container escape. "By injecting our exploit code into nvidia-persistenced, we first force the hijacked process to execute a UVM-based local privilege escalation (LPE)," the researcher said. The Q3 Vulnerability Pulse: Discovery ≠ Exploitation

threat-intel Cisco Talos · 2026-10-15 · ↗ original

Welcome to this week’s edition of the Threat Source newsletter. 

I've been following a lot of (sometimes heated) threads and discussions lately about the current vulnerability landscape. I learned new words/phrases such as Summer of Hell (although I'm writing this with 1°C/34°F outside), Vulnpocalypse, Bugmageddon, Bugpocalypse, Breachpocalypse... 

Let's look at some numbers:

We crossed the 70k mark, and it's only the end of September. This has never been seen before — I think we can agree this number is skyrocketing. Also, CVE numbering organically grew into the six-digit range. Check your tooling. Some might say we broke that barrier already in 2014 (CVE-2014-125110), or even hit seven digits in 2015 (CVE-2015-1000013), but those were allocated artificially. (History note: Before 2014, 9999 was the highest possible identifier.) 

So that could be seen as the apocalypse argument. On the other hand, we have the KEV numbers:

They didn't explode... yet? Counting by sheer volume, YoY, CVEs increased by 105%, while KEV only grew 37%. Sounds huge at first, but following a 30% increase from 2024 to 2025, that's less dramatic — a counter-argument of sorts.

CVEs from 2025 or older still account for 33%. In other words, a good third of the KEVs are suggesting there is a patch gap. Looking at the 2026 CVEs, the share where the publication date matches the date added to KEV is 29%, which is only 2% more than (all of) 2025. Acknowledging this "math" is not the whole ball of wax, but it still tells me we are not living in a zero-day apocalypse right now. There’s also a slight uptick in networking gear-related CVEs, to 27%.

This is the second time I'll ask you to check your tooling. A new column, “forensicTriage,” was added to the KEV to flag which high-risk vulnerabilities require mandatory forensic triage and incident response workflows. You can read more about it in BOD 26-04, or the Implementation Guidance. 

So where does that leave us? The CVE numbers alone — that +70k mark, now that Q3 is behind us — make a decent case for Vulnpocalypse, or whichever name you prefer. That part of the story is real. But discovery isn't exploitation, and that gap is exactly what the KEV numbers remind us of. Vendors seem to be keeping pace too, at least partially — KEV is growing, just not nearly at CVE's speed, which on its own would almost be good news. 

Where the story breaks down is on the practical side. More than a third of actively exploited vulnerabilities still trace back to 2025 or earlier. Patch debt doesn't care how many new apocalypse-themed hashtags we invent. 

So, panic? I still don't think so. Relax? Definitely not either. Somewhere between the two is where the real work sits — chipping away at that backlog, one legacy system at a time. Maybe by next quarter it'll feel less like Bugmageddon and more like business as usual. Or maybe I won't even need a warmer jacket for the upcoming "Winter of Hell."

You don’t need a nation state to warm up a fridge

red-team Pen Test Partners · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

TL;DR 

  • Over the night of the 11th of October 2026, the fridges and freezers in several hundred French supermarkets were made to warm up. Stock was binned, some equipment reportedly caught fire, and losses run to tens of millions of euros. 
  • The controllers are Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC units, many reachable directly from the internet, with a built-in login derived from the current date. 
  • That login is a code generated from the current date by an algorithm. Reaching 50C meant forcing the defrost heaters to stay on across the store’s cases, not just changing a setpoint. Both took some knowledge. 
  • No AI or insider access needed, and basic cyber hygiene would have prevented it. 

Widespread food spoilage across Europe

Over the night of the 11th of October 2026, the fridges and freezers in several hundred French supermarkets were made to warm up. Stock was spoiled across Intermarché, Netto, the U cooperative, Carrefour Market and Monoprix, with E.Leclerc also reported. The Paris prosecutor has reported fires. Losses run into tens of millions of euros, with similar reports from Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany and Belgium. 

What stands out is how little it took. 

What these controllers are 

The controllers are Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC system managers, common across European food retail. A system manager is the box Danfoss calls the brain of the store’s refrigeration. It does not cool anything itself. It coordinates the controllers that do. 

The cooling in a modern supermarket is central. One or more compressor racks, the packs, make the cold for the whole store, and Danfoss pack controllers run them, bringing compressors in and out to match demand. Large stores now run these on CO2. Each display case and cold room then has its own small controller that manages that case: its temperature and its defrost. The system manager sits on top of all of them, logging data, raising alarms, running schedules, and pushing settings down to every controller. 

It is not a thermostat with a web page. It logs temperatures, pressures, defrost cycles and compressor load continuously, and keeps the food-safety temperature records (HACCP) that retailers are legally required to hold. It tunes how hard the plant runs to save energy, and raises alarms by email and to Danfoss’s Alsense cloud. A single store has dozens of cases and cold rooms. The logging and alarms exist because nobody can walk round reading all of them every few minutes, and a slow fault or an overnight drift would be missed if they tried. 

The built-in login 

The AK-SM has a second login path that does not use the normal username and password form. It is reached by setting undocumented attributes in a request to xml.cgi, it needs no valid account, and it returns an administrative session if you supply the right code. 

The code is generated from the device’s date by a fixed algorithm, with no per-device secret, so the same code is valid on every unit for a given day. The controller will even hand out its own date to anyone who asks, without logging in, so you do not have to guess what day it thinks it is. 

Working out the code means recovering the algorithm. Two teams have done it: Synacktiv in 2023 on the SM800, SC255 and SC355, and Claroty’s Team82 on the current AK-SM 800A in 2026, where it is CVE-2025-41450. Neither published the algorithm or a working proof of concept, so it is not a copy-paste exploit today. It is also not hard to reach. With the firmware in hand, recovering it is a modest reverse-engineering job, and once recovered the same daily code opens every exposed unit and can be scripted. 

Getting a freezer to 50C 

Getting in is the easy part. Turning that into warm food, and then into a fire, takes a bit of knowledge of how the refrigeration works. 

A refrigerated case cools the air with an evaporator coil, the cold part behind the cabinet. Moisture freezes onto that coil as frost, the same as in a home freezer. Frost insulates the coil and chokes the airflow, so the case gets less efficient. To clear it, the controller runs a defrost: it stops cooling that case and switches on an electric heater on the coil for a few minutes to melt the frost, then goes back to cooling. A sensor or a timer ends the defrost once the coil is clear, so the heater is never on for long. 

Change a case’s target temperature and it drifts up to store ambient, around 20C to 25C, which is enough to ruin stock. To push past that you force the defrost to stay on and defeat the sensor and timer that end it, so the heater just keeps heating. The settings that control defrost, its method, its schedule, the termination temperature and the time limit, are ordinary parameters, described in the Danfoss manuals. 

The heater lives on the case controller, not the system manager, so the attacker is really reconfiguring dozens of case controllers, not one box. That is not one click. What makes it practical is that the system manager is built to push settings to every controller at once, and to disable the alarms at the same time so nobody is paged. So: trivial to get in, a bit of refrigeration knowledge to turn it into damage, then scriptable across the whole store. Someone who understood supermarket refrigeration, or who read the documentation, could do it. 

The fires are the part that turns spoiled stock into a safety incident. A defrost heater is built to run for short periods. Held on continuously it runs for far longer than it is meant to, and can get hotter. The heaters usually have a thermal cut-out of their own, so they should trip rather than burn. Where there is already a weak point, such as a poor electrical connection, the extra duty is what makes it overheat and smoke. That makes the fires look like a side effect of running the heaters far outside their normal duty, not something the attacker set out to cause, and not something that would happen at all sites. 

Why were they on the internet 

Whoever put these controllers online was not trying to expose a control system, but that is what happened. The refrigeration is usually fitted and maintained by a third party who needs remote access to it. A cellular modem on the unit is the quick way to give them that, quicker than asking the retailer for a managed connection. So the controller ends up on the internet, on a link the retailer often does not own, cannot see, and has no record of. The people who would normally catch an exposed management interface never get the chance. 

Who did it 

We do not know, and the barrier is low enough that it does not narrow the field much. A bored teenager could have done this. So could a state actor choosing a deniable, low-cost way to disrupt a country’s food retail. There are also reports that these controllers were attacked quietly a few years ago, so it would not be the first time. Now that it is front-page news and trivial to repeat, expect copycats within weeks. 

This is not just Danfoss 

This is not specific to one vendor. The pattern is a refrigeration or building controller with a web interface on the public internet and a built-in credential never meant to be reached from there. CISA covered both Danfoss and Copeland in the same advisory batch in August 2025. 

In separate research, now in coordinated disclosure, we found internet-exposed controllers from another vendor already compromised by a botnet. We pulled and analysed the implant. The devices are low-powered and on slow cellular links, which rules out crypto mining and makes them poor for denial-of-service. On that reasoning, what is left is access. Someone is collecting footholds on the same class of device that just spoiled a few hundred supermarkets. We will publish the detail once disclosure completes. 

Basic hygiene would have prevented it 

Two things would have changed the outcome. Cyber hygiene would have prevented the access: take the controller off the public internet, put remote access behind a VPN and an IP allowlist, bring third-party kit onto the network in a controlled, owned and documented way rather than leaving it on a contractor’s own connection, change known and default credentials, and patch to a current release. CERT-FR said the same in CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 on the 12th of October. 

There was plenty of warning 

  • 2023: Synacktiv and HackDefense published vulnerabilities in the AK-SM 800A and the end-of-life AK-EM 100, and the date-based login bypass on the SM800, SC255 and SC355. 
  • August 2025: Claroty’s Team82 published the AK-SM 800A research (CVE-2025-41450, CVE-2025-41451, CVE-2025-41452), and CISA issued ICSA-25-140-03. 
  • Danfoss removed the date-code login from release 4.2 onwards. See the Danfoss security advisories. 
  • 12th October 2026: CERT-FR issued CERTFR-2026-ALE-012. 

The access was known, the mechanism documented, and the fix available. The devices were still on the internet and still on old firmware. 

Takeaways 

If you run refrigeration, or any controller a contractor reaches remotely, assume it is on the internet until you have checked. Take it off the internet, put remote access behind a VPN and an allowlist, change known credentials, and patch. That is the baseline, not the whole job. Owning and documenting these third-party connections, monitoring them, and segmenting the network properly all sit behind it. 

None of this needed sophistication. It needed a controller on the internet and a login anyone can generate. Both are things you can find and fix today.
