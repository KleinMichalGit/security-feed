Hirudo Android Trojan Uses Patched Banking Apps to Bypass Their Built-In Security Checks

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

Cybersecurity researchers have disclosed details of a new Android banking trojan called Hirudo that works by replacing legitimate banking apps with purpose-built patched versions designed to bypass security checks from inside the app itself, marking a new escalation in financial fraud.

Dutch mobile security company ThreatFabric said it identified replacements for eight Indonesian banking apps, while credential targeting in the malware spans Indonesia, China, India, Mexico, Morocco and Thailand.

"The operation combines credential theft, device takeover, screen and camera capture, and SMS theft, in combination with a fairly mature backend," Eward Driehuis, vice president of fraud engineering at ThreatFabric, said in a statement shared with The Hacker News.

Hirudo facilitates on-device credential theft and live remote control. The malware is delivered by shopping, adult video, and entertainment dropper apps that carry the trojan as a separate installation package, which is then served as an in-app update.

Some of the dropper apps are listed below -

  • Shopora (com.agbbh.kjdrttt)
  • 1V1 X (com.onev.vip)
  • KoiShort (com.koishort.app)
  • ZoyaTV (com.zoyatv.app)

"The lure is functional, loading real content in a WebView, a storefront, a video catalogue or short dramas, so the experience looks legitimate," ThreatFabric explained. "Behind that content the dropper runs a fixed startup routine: it collects promotion-tracking data, fetches its campaign configuration, and checks whether the payload, the Hirudo RAT, is already installed with its accessibility service and notification access enabled."

If it's not present, it displays a fake in-app update prompt to trigger the installation of the malware. It's worth emphasizing that Hirudo is installed as a second app and must be granted its own permissions before it can carry out the malicious activities.

As observed in the case of StreamRat, the malware implements an anti-detection technique based on blocking Internet access by configuring a deliberately non-functional VPN connection prior to the payload installation. This is seen as an attempt to prevent other apps and services, like Google Play Protect, from carrying out cloud-based checks of the payload.

"However, Play Protect also provides offline detection for known potentially harmful applications, meaning that the mechanism does not completely disable its protection," ThreatFabric noted last month. "We therefore assess that the technique is more likely intended to reduce the effectiveness of online reputation and code-analysis mechanisms rather than bypass Play Protect entirely."

Depending on the nature of the lure app used, the dropper is also designed to serve bogus screens to capture payment PINs, serve fake screens requesting login credentials for banks and wallet apps, display fake lock screen pattern and PIN overlays, and facilitate total remote control of the device.

While all this behavior is par for the course when it comes to Android banking trojans, it's their attempts to replace the target banking app with a tampered copy that stand out. Specifically, the purpose-built replacements embed a hooking engine to blind signature, accessibility, screen-capture, and Play Integrity checks from within the app. The replacement targets eight Indonesian apps.

"For these, the RAT detects the installed banking app through accessibility, checks its signing certificate against a stored reference, [and] then shows a fake 'update' prompt," ThreatFabric explained. "It opens Android's uninstaller for the genuine app without waiting for the user to tap, and downloads the bank-specific replacement in parallel."

While the need for having to remove the app before installing its near-identical malicious copy is a "real constraint," the mechanism offers the ability to bypass the banking app's own security checks by returning responses fed by the attacker beforehand.

This involves returning the genuine app signing certificate, returning an empty list when querying for enabled accessibility services, and stripping the FLAG_SECURE flag so that the app's screens can be viewed and recorded remotely.

"Key-attestation certificates are re-signed with a bundled keybox, and Play Integrity requests are relayed over plain HTTP to an operator-controlled server, which returns a verdict for the app to accept," the mobile security company said.

"Two details are worth underlining for defenders. First, the attestation keybox the engine ships is not the operators' own: it is a publicly leaked keybox. Second, because the Integrity request is relayed rather than genuinely produced, a valid Integrity response alone is not evidence of an untampered app."

Evidence indicates that the malware is distributed via fake web pages masquerading as Google Play Store landing pages for shopping, short drama, and football apps. Unsuspecting users are assessed to be directed to these websites through ads on Meta-owned social media platforms.

The entire operation rests on a backend that combines device control with the ability to manage different campaigns and stolen data, including device records, lock-screen history, and financial profiles containing monthly income and risk-score fields.

"Hirudo marks a deliberate escalation in how Android bankers approach hardened applications," ThreatFabric said. "By moving its tampering inside the target app, the operation shifts the contest from the device to the application's own integrity checks, and from a bespoke patch to a reusable engine that can be retargeted at other apps and regions."

"Once an app can be replaced, its locally produced signals, signature, accessibility list, and integrity verdict, must be treated as attacker-influenced and verified server-side," ThreatFabric said.

Last month, the findings came a little over a month after the cybersecurity firm revealed StreamRat's targeting of Spanish-speaking users via Meta and TikTok advertisements impersonating a free TV streaming service named Streamtv Esp to enable control over compromised Android devices.

"StreamRat abuses accessibility services and MediaProjection to provide operators with near-complete control over infected devices, combining VNC and hidden-screen control, UI-tree collection, keylogging, credential-stealing overlays, internet and screen-blocking capabilities," ThreatFabric said.

An AI Agent Found a Critical OTP Bypass in Production. The Attack Was the Easy Part.

news The Hacker News · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

The bug was simple: the login let you choose where your sign-in code went. Proving it on a live website was the hard part.

TL;DR

  • During an authorized assessment, an AI pentesting agent found a critical authentication bypass on a major insurer's production website, on its own.
  • One login request let the user choose where the one-time passcode went.
  • The attack was the easy part. Proving it on a live site took web context built over a decade.

The Login Worked. Then the Agent Stopped.

Reflectiz's AI pentesting agent was mapping the login flow of a major insurer's production website, which has millions of users.

The agent requested a login code and directed it to an email inbox it controlled. The code arrived. The login went through. Its guardrails halted the test after the login succeeded.

The agent had discovered a critical-severity authentication bypass on its own. Reflectiz reported it to the insurer.

The Flaw: The Request Chose Where the Code Went

A one-time passcode (OTP) only proves who you are if it goes somewhere already linked to the account. Here, that link was broken.

The login request carried two fields that decided delivery: the channel and the address. Normally, the code went by text to the phone number on the account. But the request could change the channel. Switch it to email, and the server sent the code to whatever address the request named, not the address registered to the account.

The server never checked that the address belonged to the account.

Binding a code to its account is one of the most basic checks in authentication. OWASP's testing guide asks this in plain words: "Are the OTPs tied to the correct user account...?"

An attacker with the required customer identifiers could potentially use the flaw to access that customer's account.

From a Suspicious Field to a Proven Bypass

The agent began with reconnaissance, looking at the site's JavaScript and the API calls behind its pages. That analysis surfaced the OTP request and two values that appeared to control how and where the code was sent. Values like those can suggest a hypothesis, but they’re not a finding, since the server might reject the changes or block them later in the login. So the agent built a targeted test around both values and ran it through the live login flow, which confirmed the bypass.

Orphaned VBA Projects Inside DWG Files

threat-intel SANS ISC · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

Years ago I wrote diary entries about VBA code stored inside AutoCAD drawing files (.dwg).

In a nutshell: the VBA code is stored inside an OLE file, that is embedded inside the DWG file. You can do a brute-force search for the embedded OLE file, or you can follow a pointer inside the header of the DWG file.

I wrote a small Python script (autocad.py) for this second case. It looks at a 32-bit pointer at position 0x24 inside the DWG file. This points to a 16-byte long header, followed by the embedded file. This 16-byte long header contains the length of the embedded file. You use the script together with my tool cut-bytes.py, like this:

cut-bytes.py -S autocad.py -P AutoCAD : 461ed52aeb75384133a8238b2eb481ae5d505d4961effa622673d65a889c8218.vir.zip | oledump.py

 

This command carves the DWG file (inside the ZIP file) and extracts the embedded OLE file, and pipes it into oledump.py for analysis.

When the pointer is zero, there is no embedded OLE file, and cut-bytes.py returns nothing.

Recently, I discovered 2 DWG files that are an exception: their pointer to the embedded OLE file is zero, but if you do a brute-force search, you will find an embedded OLE file with a VBA project.

And right in front of the embedded OLE file, there is the 16-byte long header that contains the lenght of the embedded file.

Using the AutoCAD script to extract the file gives nothing:

But oledump.py can find the embedded OLE file and analyze it:

So while using oledump.py to analyze DWG files you find a VBA project embedded inside a DWG file, then also verify if it is not orphaned: verify that the pointer at 0x24 is not zero (or use the autocad.py script).

Because when a DWG file contains an orphaned VBA project, the VBA code can not have executed when someone opened the DWG file with AutoCAD. AutoCAD "does not see" orphaned VBA projects.

Notice that a default installation of AutoCAD does not support VBA. VBA for AutoCAD is a separate install.

I don't know how orphaning happens, but I did find an AutoLISP command to remove a VBA project from a DWG file: 

(dictremove (namedobjdict) "ACAD_VBA")

Maybe this dictremove command clears the pointer, but does not remove the embedded OLE file.

SAP Patches Critical NetWeaver, S/4HANA Vulnerabilities

news SecurityWeek · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

Enterprise software maker SAP on Tuesday announced the release of 22 new and updated security notes, including four that address critical-severity vulnerabilities.

At the top of the list is CVE-2026-89111 (CVSS score of 10/10), an OAuth2 SSRF vulnerability in Cloud SDK for Python that impacts multi-tenant BTP applications.

“An attacker who controls the tenant_subdomain parameter can redirect OAuth2 token requests to an attacker-controlled server, causing client credentials to be exfiltrated,” SAP explains in a GitHub advisory.

Next in line is CVE-2026-89110 (CVSS score of 9.9/10), a broken access control in certain portal functionality of NetWeaver Visual Composer.

According to enterprise application security firm Onapsis, the flaw could allow “a low-privileged attacker to invoke internal operations on a preconfigured remote system” and fully compromise it.

The third critical-severity vulnerability resolved on SAP’s October 2026 Security Patch Day is CVE-2026-89107 (CVSS score of 9.9/10), a code injection issue in S/4HANA that could allow authenticated attackers to execute arbitrary ABAP code on the system.

The security defect exists because the rule definition process fails to properly validate input, allowing attackers holding a specific business role in the Fiori Applications to submit crafted input. Successful exploitation could allow attackers to take over a vulnerable system, Onapsis explains.

Ransomware Gangs Embrace Physical Threats, Family Stalking?

news Dark Reading · 2026-10-14 · ↗ original

Ransomware actors have been threatening their victims with physical violence, according to new survey data. Many are reporting actual physical attacks being carried out in service of ransomware extortions, though hard data substantiating these claims is missing.

A combined two out of every five organizations that participated in Semperis's newly released 2026 Ransomware Risk Report indicated they had faced intimidation and threats associated with their ransomware attacks, and often severe ones. Even more remarkably, more than half of respondents claimed that attackers "carried through on threats" they made.

Semperis's study relied on self-reporting, and it couldn't corroborate these alarming figures, nor could Dark Reading. If true, the data suggests that ransomware is escaping the digital realm and becoming a genuine risk to victims' safety. Unless and until proven cases of violent attacks surface, however, the data must be met with caution.

Related:AI Scramble Drives Cybersecurity M&A Boom

Orgs Say Employees Are Being Assaulted

It was last year when the realization first hit: Semperis started asking survey participants about physical threats to their organizations in its 2025 annual report, and a full 40% of participants said yes, they had received such threats from their hackers.

This year's data casts some confusion around the matter, as only 17% of 2026 respondents told surveyors that they'd been subject to physical threats. A further 23% reported psychological intimidation.

Among personal threats, threatening texts, letters, or packages (57% of those who reported being threatened in the first place) were most commonly experienced. Attackers also went much further, though, by threatening employees' family and friends (37%), physical intrusion into an organization's facilities (19%), abduction (17%), bodily harm (13%), and stalking (6%).

Worst of all, perhaps, is that 53% of survey respondents reported that their attackers actually carried out their threats.

Interestingly, these figures varied significantly between countries and industry verticals. In the UK, Semperis found that around 34% of ransomware groups' threats were carried out, and that victims might expect to be stalked 18% of the time. Hackers operating in Italy are exceptionally violent, apparently, as 32% of victims there were threatened with abduction and 22% with bodily harm, with 74% claiming that their threats were carried out.

Attackers have been finding other ways to make ransomware personal issues for their victims, too. According to Semperis, victims or their families were doxxed, swatted, or subjected to some other sort of public shaming in more than half of all cases.

Related:Device Code Phishing Up 1,500% in 2026; Vishing Doubles

Are Threat Actors Actually Physically Harming People?

Asked whether ransomware victims are actually being physically harmed, Jeff Wichman, Semperis’s director of incident response, states, "Yes. In some way, shape, or form, it does happen."

In reporting this story, however, Dark Reading failed to find even a single documented case of violence perpetrated in service of a ransomware attack. Asked for a case study, Semperis pointed to a Europol press release regarding so-called "violence-as-a-service," but Europol did not connect violence-as-a-service with ransomware. However, there is hard evidence that violence in cyberattacks exists in related ways.

First, physical violence is rife and increasing around cryptocurrency thefts. Back in February, Web3 security company CertiK Skynet published its annual report on so-called "wrench attacks." As the name implies, "wrench attack" is the cryptocurrency world's term to describe when attackers physically assault their targets in order to steal their cryptocurrency wallet private keys. CertiK Skynet documented 72 cases in 2025, which represented a 75% rise over 2024. Chainalysis found a similar "surge" of wrench attacks in 2025. Halfway through 2026, it found that violent attackers had successfully won $30 million worth of crypto.

Related:Stronger AI Safety Requires Peeking Inside the 'Black Box'

What's true of cryptocurrency is not necessarily true of enterprise cyberattacks, of course. But cyber extortion groups do appear to dabble in physical intrusion. In spring 2026, the FBI and Mandiant researchers each documented the "Silent Ransom Group" posing as IT technicians to enter corporate offices and extract data from endpoints using USBs.

Lastly, it does seem the case that threats of violence are rising across online attacks generally (if not ransomware specifically). In its Internet Crime Report 2025, the FBI reported having received 1,360 complaints involving threats of violence, in cases that did not involve extortion. In 2025 it received 4,826 — a 250% rise year over year.

Why Hackers Turn to Physical Threats

Semperis has had firsthand experience with ransomware groups' threats. A few years ago, while representing a US government organization in a ransom negotiation, one of its own employees received a package on his doorstep with a note "alluding to physical violence if he didn't back off," the BBC reported.

Ironically, a rise in physical threats could be interpreted as a byproduct of improved organizational cybersecurity. "As attackers lose leverage with [extortion] — companies now are in the position of 'we've got this and this covering us' — now it's more about forcing that demand. 'You need to talk to us. We are serious,'" Wichman says.

"It will keep evolving just like anything else," he continues. "I look at it kind of like the days of the mafia. Originally, it was just about extorting money from businesses. Then it became: You didn't pay us, now we're going to get violent. Same old, same old."

Tim Beasley, a senior incident response consultant at Semperis, was investigating a cyberattack involving Russia’s Midnight Blizzard when a package containing nesting dolls and other regional gifts showed up on his doorstep. At the time, Beasley served as the lead investigator for Microsoft’s Detection and Response Team (DART). He assumed it was from the group, which has been linked to the Kremlin’s Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), although the sender didn’t leave a note. “Just little souvenirs and gift basket stuff from that area of the world,” he told The Register. “We-know-where-you-are kind of stuff. Some groups even use cybercrime as a gateway drug to real-life violence. Over the summer, Europol and its partners' investigators flagged 4,340 “horrific” URLs for removal as part of an ongoing crackdown on The Com (short for community), a loosely knit network of online groups whose young members participate in a range of illicit activities including hacking, swatting, digital extortion, real-life shootings, and other physical violence. “We’ve seen violence as a service get used,” Beasley said. If you feel like you're under duress, then by all means get law enforcement involved. Don't be afraid to call the cops. Don't be afraid to get the FBI involved, sooner rather than later. And don’t pay them.” This last piece of advice is important, as once you pay, they're going to come back for more. Microsoft Patches More Than 1,400 Security Flaws

news Krebs on Security · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

Microsoft today issued updates to fix more than 1,400 security vulnerabilities in its Windows operating systems and other software. Today’s epic patch batch dwarfs September’s record of nearly 1,000 fixes, which was already about ten times the normal monthly patch load from Redmond.

Just one of the weaknesses patched this week is considered a “zero day,” or already exploited in the wild. CVE-2026-96951 is a security feature bypass in the Windows Shell that affects Windows 10 and 11 systems, as well as some Server versions.

Windows 10 users might be wondering if they can still expect security updates after this month. Microsoft had been slated to stop shipping updates for Windows 10 systems in October 2025, but ended up adding another year of free updates after considerable pushback. A few months ago, Microsoft quietly extended that support for another year (until October 2027).

At least 78 of the flaws fixed this month are rated “critical,” meaning they could be exploited with little or no action by the user, and most of those critical bugs allow an attacker to execute code of their choice on the vulnerable system.

While Microsoft said there are no signs anyone is exploiting it yet, a security feature bypass in the .NET Framework (CVE-2026-108398) was publicly disclosed prior to today, potentially giving attackers advance time to work out how to exploit the bug.

Satnam Narang at Tenable said the number of flaws fixed this month far surpasses the previous record-setting year 2020, when Microsoft patched 1,261 CVEs.

“We’ve now crossed the 4,000 CVE mark with two months left to go,” he said.

Microsoft credits large language models for the high volume of bug discovery, and presumably AI gets credit for many of the patches used to fix these flaw. Meanwhile, security experts have been warning that these unusually large patch volleys from Microsoft are placing extra strain on corporate security teams, which often have to test individual updates before deploying them across a network to ensure the fixes don’t cause problems for other software.

Tyler Reguly, associate director of security research and development at Fortra, notes that just 26 Microsoft vulnerabilities appeared in the “Known Exploited Vulnerabilities” (KEV) catalog maintained by the U.S. Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) so far this year, and security teams would do well to prioritize patching those 26 vulnerabilities first.

“Security leaders should focus on exploitable risk, and ask whether their teams have the clarity, capacity and support to act,” Reguly said.

Researchers have said several government agencies effectively fund themselves by employing their own coterie of IT workers. Many IT workers are located in China or Russia but a recent United Nations report found hundreds of North Korean IT workers in Laos, Cambodia and several other countries.  

Changing tactics

Assistant FBI Director Brett Leatherman said North Korea “continues to deploy remote IT workers using stolen identities to infiltrate American companies and generate revenue for the regime and its weapons programs.” 

Leatherman noted that the FBI released a new public service announcement describing the latest tactics used by North Korean IT workers to recruit Americans and perpetrate the scheme. 

The report notes that North Korea is now taking significant steps to counter efforts by U.S. companies to stop the scheme. The scam operations are now subcontracting entire jobs to third-parties in South Asia or West Africa and hiring facilitators to conduct a wider array of tasks. 

“In addition to remote labor, [North Koreans] hire South Asian or West African freelancers to assist with applying to jobs and with getting hired. [They] pay these freelancers to apply manually to as many open positions as possible over a given time-period, or to conduct interviews on their behalf,” the FBI advisory said.

“Increasingly, North Koreans are obtaining jobs with western companies and then hiring freelancers to complete work assignments for a fraction of the total salary. [They] subcontract employment-related remote labor to third-party freelancers, usually in Pakistan, India, Nigeria, or neighboring countries.”

North Korea is also targeting low-income Americans, offering freelance gigs with mundane requests like hosting laptops or interviewing on someone’s behalf. The FBI said it has seen North Korean IT workers strategize on the best locations to connect with Americans “facing financial stress.”

Those behind the scheme typically claim to be Chinese or East Asian nationals needing temporary U.S. presence or purporting to be Eastern Europeans displaced by the Russian-Ukraine war. 

North Koreans are “paying Americans for an expanding range of services to hide their own involvement in the scheme, including asking some to appear on camera, participate in job interviews or meetings, verify fraudulent identities, provide access to personal social media or professional networking accounts, ship packages, complete urinalysis tests or fingerprint submissions, or keep employment-related devices connected to the Internet from personal residences.”

The FBI warned that when North Koreans have been caught perpetrating the scheme, they use threats of physical violence to demand payment. Others have threatened to leak sensitive corporate data if they are not paid large lump sums. 

“[North Koreans] have hired U.S.-based facilitators to appear in-person at victim companies or at the residences of laptop farm operators for intimidation,” the FBI said. 

“Facilitators paid by [North Korean IT workers] have also made in-person threats against companies that removed ITW-related accounts.”

The advisory added that North Koreans are seeking to use identities that would allow them to apply for jobs that require U.S. government security clearances. 

IT workers “target active-duty U.S. military members to host laptops,” the FBI said. At least one active duty member of the U.S. Army has already been convicted and sentenced for allowing his identity to be used by North Korean IT workers.

Microsoft Patches for October 2026

Before we get into this month’s Microsoft release, let’s talk about what they changed. Microsoft is introducing “risk cohorts”. According to Microsoft, “They are an exploratory way to help customers organize their own analysis. They are not official Microsoft vulnerability categories, do not change Microsoft update guidance, and do not replace the assessment of individual CVEs.” Back in my day, we called these security bulletins. Now on to our regularly scheduled analysis.

Well, it’s a new record release from Microsoft, again. This is also the first four-digit volume we have ever seen and includes six-digit CVEs. As always, counting this load is tricky, but I see 1,415 new CVEs from Microsoft. That puts this month at a greater volume than any previous YEAR from Microsoft. I’m starting to think I don’t like this new normal. As for the products affected by this release, we have Windows and Windows components, Office and Office Components, Developer Tools and .NET, SharePoint, Linux kernel, Skype for Business and Microsoft Lync, SQL Server and Dynamics, Azure and cloud services, and Exchange server.

Still, if there’s solace to be had, it’s in the fact that only one of these bugs is under active attack. If you go back to the “Hot 0-day Summer” of 2023, there were 17 Microsoft 0-days between January and October as opposed to 15 this year during the same period. The total number of Microsoft CVEs in 2023? Just 914. So, AI is certainly helping in bug discovery (and likely in bug patching), but we haven’t seen the same increase in the exploitation of 0-days – yet. As witnessed at the last Pwn2Own, AI-assisted exploitation is most certainly a thing. It just hasn’t hit the mainstream yet, even though other incidents are on the rise.

Hundreds of CVEs in a single release used to be the exception; now it’s the baseline. Is AI to blame? Partly. It’s accelerating bug-hunting on both sides of the fence, so vendors are finding (and fixing) more flaws, and researchers are finding them faster too. But AI isn’t creating these bugs out of thin air. It’s just shortening the time between a vulnerability existing and someone noticing it. What matters is that discovery is scaling faster than exploitation, so teams that triage by sheer CVE count instead of exploitability are burning cycles on the wrong bugs. Patch what’s proven, prioritized, and public, and treat everything else as this month’s new normal.

Two bugs in this release are listed as publicly known: CVE-2026-108398, a .NET Framework security feature bypass that could let an attacker evade Windows Defender Application Control (WDAC) enforcement, and CVE-2026-92707, a denial-of-service bug in MessagePack-CSharp that was released by a third party and is now included in Microsoft’s release. Neither is listed as under active attack. As always, we’ll start with the bug under active attack and move on from there.

-              CVE-2026-96951 - Windows Shell Security Feature Bypass Vulnerability
This is the lone bug listed as under active attack, and it’s certainly a type of bug we’ve seen before. Going back to 2024, attackers have used multiple methods to avoid Mark of the Web (MotW) protections. This time, it’s Shell instead of SmartScreen, but the result is the same. As with any bug under active attack, test and deploy this patch quickly

-              CVE-2026-102866 – Microsoft Office SharePoint Elevation of Privilege Vulnerability
There are lots of SharePoint bugs this month, but this one stands out. It’s the only Critical-rated SharePoint CVE (CVSS 8.1), and the only one an unauthenticated attacker can exploit. It’s an improper cryptographic signature verification bug. An attacker who obtains a valid proof token for a targeted user could modify it, submit it to the server, and authenticate as that user. The only caveat is that the attacker has to obtain that token first, but there are multiple methods this could be achieved.

-              CVE-2026-65674 – Microsoft SQL Server Remote Code Execution Vulnerability
Like SharePoint, there’s lots to choose from in the SQL component. This one is the highest-scoring CVSS at 8.8 and has the best exploitability rating of any SQL Server bug this month. It’s a deserialization of untrusted data. An authenticated attacker connects to an affected SQL Server and sends a specially crafted request to run code on the server. No user interaction is needed. Definitely check this on any SQL Server directly connected to the internet.

-              CVE-2026-70571 - Microsoft Exchange Server Remote Code Execution Vulnerability
Technically speaking, this is a wormable bug in Exchange Server. However, I wouldn’t count on that happening. Microsoft states, “Exploitation requires an attacker to have a deep understanding of the target environment and is not guaranteed to succeed.” While the wording is obtuse, it generally means the likelihood of stars aligning to exploit the Exchange Server through this bug is minuscule.

Local Privilege Escalations: To say these bugs are prevalent is an understatement. However, of the 677 EoP bugs in the data, 606 (90%) lead to SYSTEM, administrator or root privileges. There are also a few integrity-level or sandbox escapes. There are seven bugs where the advisory says only “ELEVATED privileges,” or describes a cloud identity or data-access issue. There are nearly two dozen others where the attacker runs as another user or service account, creates or deletes files in a privileged context, or reaches Local Service. This includes CVE-2026-70190, which lets the attacker create, modify or delete files as SYSTEM but doesn’t give SYSTEM privileges. Ivanti warns of Connect Secure zero-day exploited in attacks

news BleepingComputer · 2026-10-13 · ↗ original

Ivanti urged customers on Tuesday to immediately patch an actively exploited zero-day vulnerability in the Ivanti Connect Secure (ICS) remote access gateway.

Formerly known as Pulse Connect Secure and originally Juniper Pulse, the enterprise-grade ICS SSL VPN provides remote employees, contractors, and hybrid workforces with secure access to corporate and cloud resources.

Tracked as CVE-2026-104935, this high-severity authentication bypass zero-day affects ICS versions 22.8R2.4 and earlier, 25.1.2.2 and earlier, and 25.1.1.1 and earlier.

Remote threat actors without privileges can exploit CVE-2026-104935 in low-complexity attacks that don't require user interaction to execute arbitrary commands on targeted ICS appliances.

"At the time of disclosure, Ivanti is aware of limited exploitation in the wild. We are working directly with those organizations while making information, updates and remediation available to the broader customer community. If you are running one of the impacted versions, Ivanti recommends you act immediately," Ivanti said.

"Customers should review the security advisory, upgrade to the fixed version as soon as possible, and run the Integrity Checker Tool regularly."

Internet threat watchdog Shadowserver now tracks over 5,500 ICS instances exposed online, but there is no information on how many are honeypots or have already been secured against ongoing attacks.

Exploited since at least October 1

Cybersecurity firm Volexity, which found and reported the flaw, revealed earlier today that a previously unknown threat actor it tracks as UTA0576 has chained the CVE-2026-104935 vulnerability since at least October 3 with two other flaws to achieve unauthenticated root-level code execution on fully patched devices.

"Notably, UTA0576 deployed no malware or web shell. Instead, the threat actor re-exploited the device for every command it ran and tampered with logs to cover its tracks, evading Ivanti’s Integrity Checker Tool," Volexity said.

While Ivanti has patched two flaws, it is still working to address a privilege escalation vulnerability that UTA0576 exploited as part of the same chain.

Threat intelligence company Defused also confirmed Volexity's report, saying that it caught the first signs of CVE-2026-104935 exploitation in the wild starting October 1.

​Hackers have frequently exploited Ivanti vulnerabilities, often as zero-days, because they provide a quick way to breach enterprise networks and steal sensitive customer and corporate data.

Ivanti has also patched several other Connect Secure zero-days exploited in the wild over the past several years, some of them chained in widespread attacks to deploy malware.

It addressed an authentication bypass (CVE-2023-46805) and a command injection (CVE-2024-21887) in early January 2024, and a server-side request forgery (CVE-2024-21893) two weeks later.

More recently, it fixed a remote code execution (RCE) vulnerability tracked as CVE-2025-0282 in January 2025 and a second RCE flaw (CVE-2025-22457) that China-linked cyber spies have exploited since at least mid-March 2025.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) has flagged 36 vulnerabilities across various Ivanti products as actively exploited in the wild since November 2021, 12 of which ransomware gangs have also abused.
